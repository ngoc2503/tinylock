Every child develops at their own pace. Some children start talking early, while others take longer. Some enjoy playing with other children, while others prefer playing alone. However, if a child consistently shows limited social interaction, does not respond to their name, rarely shares attention with others, or engages in repetitive behaviors, parents may want to observe more closely.

These may be signs associated with autism spectrum disorder (ASD).

How can parents recognize possible signs of autism? Which behaviors should they look for at different ages? And what should they do if they have concerns about their child's development?

## 1. What is autism?

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects how a child communicates, interacts socially, learns, and responds to the surrounding environment.

It is called a "spectrum" because every autistic child is different and may need different levels and types of support. Some children have significant difficulties with spoken communication, while others speak fluently but find it difficult to maintain conversations or understand social cues. Some children may have notable strengths in particular areas while experiencing challenges in others.

Autism is generally characterized by two main areas:

- **Differences in social communication and interaction:** A child may have difficulty using eye contact, facial expressions, gestures, or spoken language to communicate, share emotions, and interact with others.
- **Restricted or repetitive behaviors, interests, or activities:** A child may prefer repetitive activities, have difficulty adapting to changes, or respond differently to sounds, lights, smells, tastes, and textures.

Not every autistic child shows the same characteristics. Some signs may become noticeable in the first years of life, while others become more apparent as children grow and face increasingly complex social and learning demands.

## 2. Common signs that a child may be autistic

Parents should pay attention to behaviors in the two main areas below.

### Area 1: Differences in social communication and interaction

These are often among the first differences parents notice.

**Limited eye contact**

A child may make less eye contact when being called, playing, or interacting with others. However, limited eye contact alone does not mean a child is autistic. It should be considered alongside other communication and interaction skills.

**Not responding consistently to their name**

When called, a child may not turn around, look toward the person calling, or respond consistently, even when the environment is relatively quiet and the child is not focused on another activity.

If a child does not respond to sounds or spoken words, hearing difficulties should also be assessed.

**Limited use of gestures**

A child may rarely or never:
- Point to request something or share interest.
- Wave goodbye.
- Nod or shake their head to answer.
- Show objects to other people.
- Bring an object to an adult to share interest.

The important question is not simply whether a child can point, but whether they use gestures to share attention and connect with other people.

**Limited sharing of enjoyment and interests**

When seeing an interesting toy, animal, or new event, a child may not look toward a parent, point it out, or try to share the experience.

For example, a child may see an airplane in the sky and watch it alone without pointing or trying to draw an adult's attention to it.

**Limited back-and-forth interaction**

A child may rarely participate in interactive games such as peekaboo, clapping games, or turn-taking activities. When an adult smiles, talks, or expresses emotions, the child may respond less often or have difficulty maintaining the interaction.

**Difficulty understanding emotions and social cues**

As children grow older, they may have difficulty recognizing facial expressions, emotions, body language, social rules, or cues that indicate whether someone wants to continue or end a conversation.

### Area 2: Repetitive behaviors, restricted interests, and sensory differences

**Repeating certain movements**

A child may frequently:
- Flap their hands or move their wrists or fingers repeatedly.
- Rock their body.
- Spin around or run in repetitive patterns.
- Walk on their toes.
- Arrange objects in a particular order.

Some repetitive behaviors also occur in children who are not autistic. Their frequency, intensity, context, and impact on daily life are important.

**Playing with objects in a fixed or repetitive way**

Instead of using toys in different ways, a child may:
- Focus mainly on spinning toy car wheels.
- Line up objects in rows.
- Open and close doors or drawers repeatedly.
- Focus on a small part of an object rather than its overall function.

**Having highly focused or restricted interests**

A child may spend a great deal of time exploring one topic, object, or activity. They may want to talk or play about that interest frequently and find it difficult to move on to another activity.

**Difficulty adapting to changes**

A child may become very upset when:
- Their daily routine changes.
- A familiar route is different.
- Objects are moved to another location.
- They have to stop a favorite activity.
- Someone changes the way they play or arrange objects.

**Different responses to sounds, lights, and sensory experiences**

Some children are highly sensitive to vacuum cleaners, traffic noise, bright lights, clothing tags, food textures, or certain smells. They may cover their ears, cry, avoid the stimulus, or become distressed.

Other children may show less response to certain sounds, temperatures, or touch. They may enjoy watching spinning objects, looking at lights, or repeatedly touching objects.

## 3. Signs of autism at different ages

The milestones below are useful observations, not diagnostic criteria. Children may acquire skills at different times.

### Children aged 0–12 months

During the first year, parents can observe whether their child:
- Makes limited eye contact with caregivers or responds less often during interactions.
- Rarely smiles back when an adult smiles.
- Makes few babbling sounds or has limited back-and-forth vocal interaction.
- Does not respond to their name by around 9 months.
- Shows limited facial expressions or shared attention with caregivers.

None of these behaviors alone confirms autism. At this age, parents should consider overall development and discuss concerns with a healthcare professional.

### Children aged 12–24 months

Communication and social interaction differences may become more noticeable during this period.

Parents should pay attention if their child:
- Uses few gestures, such as waving, pointing, or showing objects.
- Does not respond consistently to their name.
- Rarely imitates adults' actions.
- Does not actively share attention with parents.
- Rarely participates in interactive games.
- Does not point to show others something interesting by around 18 months.
- Uses fewer ways to communicate through words and gestures than expected for their age.
- Repeats certain actions or becomes very distressed when routines change.

It is especially important to observe social communication, rather than simply counting how many words a child can say.

### Children aged 2–3 years

At this age, children usually begin using language to request, share, and interact with others. Possible signs of concern include:

- Rarely initiating communication or having difficulty maintaining back-and-forth interaction.
- Not consistently combining words, eye contact, and gestures to communicate.
- Preferring solitary play and showing limited interest in sharing activities.
- Engaging in little pretend play, such as pretending to cook, care for a doll, or act out roles.
- Repeating words or phrases without using them flexibly in context.
- Having significant difficulty accepting changes in daily activities.
- Showing repetitive behaviors or noticeably different sensory responses.

### Children aged 3–5 years

As children begin attending preschool and playing with peers, social communication differences may become easier to notice.

A child may:
- Have difficulty joining group play or taking turns.
- Rarely initiate friendships or struggle to maintain them.
- Find it difficult to understand social rules.
- Talk extensively about personal interests without noticing the listener's responses.
- Struggle to adapt to new schedules, activities, or environments.
- Show repetitive behaviors or highly focused interests.

### Children aged 6–11 years

At school age, some autistic children may speak fluently and perform well academically but still experience social communication difficulties.

Parents and teachers may notice that a child:
- Has difficulty understanding implied meanings, jokes, or figurative language.
- Struggles to maintain a two-way conversation.
- Finds it difficult to recognize peers' feelings and perspectives.
- Has trouble with group work or team activities.
- Relies heavily on predictable routines and becomes distressed by changes.
- Has highly focused interests and spends extensive time on one topic.
- Is sensitive to noise, light, smells, or crowded environments.

Some children learn to imitate their peers' communication styles to fit in. Their differences may therefore be less obvious from the outside, even though social activities can leave them exhausted.

## 4. Warning signs parents should pay particular attention to

Parents should consider discussing their concerns with a doctor or developmental specialist if they notice:

- The child does not respond to their name by around 9–12 months.
- The child uses few communication gestures, such as pointing or waving, by around 12 months.
- The child does not actively share attention or point to show something interesting by around 18 months.
- The child has limited back-and-forth interaction, imitation, or social engagement.
- Repetitive behaviors, restricted interests, or sensory differences significantly affect daily life.
- The child has noticeable delays in several developmental areas or persistent social communication difficulties.

**One particularly important warning sign is losing skills the child previously had.**

For example, a child may have used several words but later stop using them, or may have previously pointed, communicated, or interacted but gradually lose those abilities.

This requires prompt medical and developmental evaluation. Parents should not simply wait and hope the skills will return on their own.

## 5. Does delayed speech mean a child has autism?

No. Delayed speech does not automatically mean autism.

Children may have delayed speech for many reasons, including:
- Hearing difficulties.
- Language development disorders.
- Global developmental delays.
- Certain neurological or developmental conditions.
- Limited opportunities for language interaction and practice.

An important distinction is that autistic children typically have differences not only in language but also in social communication and reciprocal interaction.

For example:
- A child with delayed speech may still point to request something, follow an adult's pointing gesture, bring toys to show others, and enjoy sharing experiences. This may represent a different developmental profile from that of an autistic child.
- A child may use many words but rarely use language to communicate, pay little attention to others' responses, and experience social interaction difficulties. This child may also need an autism evaluation.

These are illustrative examples, not a reliable way to diagnose or rule out autism. A comprehensive developmental assessment is necessary.

## 6. What should parents do if they suspect autism?

### Step 1: Observe and record the child's behavior

Parents can make notes about:
- Does the child respond when called by name?
- Do they look, point, or show objects to others?
- Can they imitate actions?
- Do they engage in interactive or pretend play?
- Which behaviors do they repeat?
- How do they respond to sounds, lights, and changes in routine?
- Have they lost any previously acquired skills?

Short videos of everyday interactions can also help a doctor understand the child's behavior.

### Step 2: Arrange a developmental evaluation and screening

If you have concerns, take your child to a pediatrician, developmental specialist, or a healthcare facility experienced in child development and mental health.

The healthcare professional may:
- Ask about pregnancy, birth, and developmental history.
- Observe how the child communicates, plays, and interacts.
- Use age-appropriate developmental or autism screening tools.
- Check hearing and assess other developmental concerns when necessary.
- Refer the child to specialists for a more comprehensive evaluation.

### Step 3: Understand the M-CHAT-R/F screening tool

M-CHAT-R/F is an autism screening tool commonly used for young children aged approximately 16–30 months.

It helps identify children who may need further evaluation. It does not confirm whether a child is autistic.

- A high-risk screening result does not mean the child has been diagnosed with autism.
- A low-risk result does not completely rule out autism if parents continue to notice concerning signs.
- Do not use an online questionnaire alone to determine a child's diagnosis.

### Step 4: Do not delay developmental support

If a child has difficulties with communication, language, movement, or social interaction, parents should discuss appropriate support with qualified professionals.

Depending on the child's needs, support may include:
- Developmental intervention and communication support.
- Speech and language therapy.
- Occupational therapy for daily living skills or sensory-related difficulties.
- Parent coaching on how to interact with and support the child at home.
- Support with inclusion and learning at school.

Children do not necessarily need to wait for a formal autism diagnosis before receiving support for identified developmental difficulties.

## 7. How can parents support their child at home?

### Increase direct interaction

Spend time playing with your child, follow their interests, and create opportunities for them to initiate communication. Parents can sit at the child's level, name objects, describe actions, and wait for a response.

Do not force a child to make eye contact if they are uncomfortable.

### Encourage different ways of communicating

Children may communicate through spoken words, gestures, pictures, or assistive communication devices. Respond to and encourage their appropriate attempts to communicate.

Do not require a child to speak before responding to their needs.

### Play turn-taking and imitation games

Parents can roll a ball back and forth, build blocks together, play peekaboo, or imitate the child's sounds and actions. These activities create opportunities to learn reciprocal interaction.

### Maintain predictable routines

Some children feel safer when they know what will happen next. Parents can use pictures, simple schedules, or advance warnings before changing activities.

### Respect the child's sensory needs

If a child is uncomfortable with noise, light, or certain clothing, try to understand the cause and adjust the environment. Avoid deliberately exposing the child to overwhelming sensory experiences.

### Work with teachers and specialists

Share observations from home and school so that everyone understands the child's needs, strengths, and challenges. Support goals should be specific, appropriate for the child, and adjusted as the child progresses.

## 8. Common misconceptions about autism

**Autism is not caused by poor parenting.** It is a complex neurodevelopmental condition and should not be blamed on parents being insufficiently affectionate, talking too little, or being too permissive.

**Not every autistic child is non-speaking.** Some children do not use spoken language, some speak but have social communication difficulties, and others use language fluently while still showing autistic characteristics.

**Not every autistic child avoids eye contact.** Autism presents differently in different children. A single behavior cannot establish a diagnosis.

**Do not wait until a child is older to seek an evaluation.** Early identification and support can help children develop skills and access appropriate services.

**Do not try unproven treatments on your own.** Be cautious about products, restrictive diets, or methods advertised as guaranteed cures for autism.

## 9. When should parents seek an evaluation?

Arrange a developmental assessment if you notice one or more concerning signs, especially if they occur consistently, persist over time, or affect communication and daily activities.

You do not need to wait until a child shows every possible sign before seeking help.

If a child loses previously acquired language, communication, or movement skills, contact a healthcare professional promptly. If the child has a seizure, severe breathing difficulty, or another medical emergency, seek emergency care immediately.

## 10. Frequently asked questions

### At what age can autism be recognized?

Some signs may be noticed around 12–18 months or even earlier. Many children can receive a reliable diagnosis at around 2 years of age from an experienced professional. However, recognition and evaluation may occur later in some children.

### Can a child who makes normal eye contact still be autistic?

Yes. Eye contact is only one aspect of social interaction. Autistic children do not necessarily avoid eye contact. A comprehensive assessment should consider communication, interaction, behavior, and developmental history.

### Does a child who prefers playing alone have autism?

Not necessarily. Some children are naturally independent or need time to play alone. What matters is whether the child has persistent difficulties with shared attention, reciprocal interaction, and age-appropriate social activities.

### Can autism be completely cured?

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition and should not be presented as something that can be guaranteed to disappear completely. However, appropriate support can help children develop communication, daily living, and learning skills and participate more fully in life. Each child has different support needs.

## Conclusion

Recognizing possible signs of autism does not mean parents should diagnose their child themselves. The important steps are to observe development, pay attention to social communication, repetitive behaviors, and any loss of previously acquired skills.

If you have concerns, talk to a healthcare professional and seek a developmental evaluation as early as possible. Do not wait for delayed speech or behavioral differences to resolve on their own before seeking support.

Every child has individual strengths, needs, and developmental patterns. Understanding, acceptance, and appropriate support can give children more opportunities to grow and participate in life in ways that work best for them.
