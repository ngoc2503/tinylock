Teething is an exciting developmental milestone for many parents. However, not every baby gets their first tooth at exactly six months. Some babies develop their first tooth as early as four months, while others may not have any teeth until close to their first birthday.

So, when do babies usually start teething? How can parents recognize possible delayed tooth eruption? And what should they do if their child is growing older but still has no teeth?

This article explains the typical baby tooth eruption schedule, signs that may need attention, and how to care for your child's teeth.

## 1. When do babies usually start teething?

Most babies get their first tooth at around **six months of age**. Many begin teething between six and twelve months, although the timing can vary from one child to another.

The first teeth to appear are usually the two lower front teeth, called the lower central incisors. Other teeth gradually follow in the upper and lower jaws.

Some babies may start teething at around four months, while others may still have no teeth at twelve months. This does not necessarily indicate a problem, especially if the child is otherwise growing and developing well.

Most children have all 20 primary teeth by approximately two and a half to three years of age.

## 2. Baby teeth eruption schedule

Baby teeth generally appear in a predictable order, but the exact timing varies.

| Type of tooth | Lower jaw | Upper jaw |
|---|---:|---:|
| Central incisors | 6–10 months | 8–12 months |
| Lateral incisors | 10–16 months | 9–13 months |
| First molars | 14–18 months | 13–19 months |
| Canines | 17–23 months | 16–22 months |
| Second molars | 23–31 months | 25–33 months |

**Important:** These are average eruption ranges, not strict deadlines. Teeth may appear earlier or later, and the sequence may vary slightly between children.

By around three years of age, most children have all 20 primary teeth: ten in the upper jaw and ten in the lower jaw.

## 3. Signs that your baby may be teething

As a tooth moves toward the surface of the gum, your baby may show some of these signs:

- **Increased drooling:** Your baby may produce more saliva than usual.
- **Chewing or biting objects:** Babies may put their hands, toys, or safe objects in their mouths to chew.
- **Swollen or red gums:** The gum around an emerging tooth may become slightly swollen or sensitive.
- **Irritability:** Some babies become fussier or have more difficulty sleeping.
- **Changes in feeding:** Your baby may temporarily feed less because the gums feel uncomfortable.
- **Wanting gum massage:** Gentle massage with a clean finger may help relieve discomfort.

Not every baby experiences all these symptoms. Some children develop teeth with very few noticeable signs.

**Remember:** A temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, significant diarrhea, vomiting, or unusual lethargy should not automatically be attributed to teething. These symptoms may indicate another illness and should be assessed appropriately.

## 4. How can parents recognize possible delayed tooth eruption?

Delayed tooth eruption means that teeth appear later than the usual age range. However, there is no single age that can be used to diagnose every child.

Parents can pay attention to the following situations:

### A baby aged 6–9 months has no teeth

This is usually not a reason for serious concern. Many healthy babies have not developed their first tooth during this period.

Continue monitoring your baby's overall growth, nutrition, and routine health checkups.

### A child reaches 12 months without any teeth

Some children still have no teeth on their first birthday. This may fall within normal developmental variation.

However, parents should mention this at the next pediatric or dental visit, especially if there is a family history of late teething or other developmental concerns.

### A child reaches 18 months without any primary teeth

At this stage, parents should arrange an appointment with a pediatric dentist rather than simply waiting longer.

The dentist can examine the gums and jaw and assess tooth development. Depending on the findings, an X-ray may be considered to check whether tooth buds are present.

### Teeth appear unevenly or there are unusual gaps

A dental assessment may also be appropriate if:

- One side of the jaw has teeth while the other side remains noticeably delayed for a long time.
- Teeth appear in unusual positions or seem to be blocked from emerging.
- A tooth does not appear for an unusually long time while other teeth have erupted.
- The child has abnormalities involving the gums, jaw, or tooth shape.

Not every difference in eruption pattern indicates a medical problem. A dentist will consider the child's age, eruption sequence, and individual oral development.

## 5. Why might a child develop teeth later than expected?

Several factors may influence when a child's teeth appear.

### 1. Genetics

If a parent or close family member developed teeth later than average, the child may have a similar eruption pattern.

Family history is one of the factors a dentist may ask about when evaluating delayed eruption.

### 2. Premature birth or low birth weight

Some premature babies may have developmental timelines that differ from those of full-term babies. Healthcare professionals may consider corrected age and the child's overall health when evaluating developmental milestones.

### 3. Nutritional or health problems

Poor nutrition or certain health conditions may affect general growth and tooth development.

However, delayed teething alone cannot establish that a child has calcium deficiency, vitamin D deficiency, or a shortage of any particular nutrient.

### 4. Hormonal disorders or certain congenital conditions

In less common cases, delayed tooth eruption may be associated with hypothyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, or conditions affecting the development of teeth and jaw bones.

### 5. Missing tooth buds or an obstruction

Some children may be born without one or more developing tooth buds. In other cases, a tooth may be prevented from erupting by jaw structure, tooth position, or developmental abnormalities.

Only an appropriate professional examination and investigations can help identify the cause. Parents should not diagnose the condition based on eruption timing alone.

## 6. What should parents do if their child has delayed teething?

If you notice that your child's teeth are appearing later than expected, consider the following steps.

### Step 1: Monitor your child's development

Keep track of:
- The age when the first tooth appears.
- The number of teeth that have erupted.
- The position and order of the teeth.
- Any unusual changes in the gums or jaw.
- Your child's feeding, growth, and general health.

This information can help the dentist assess your child more accurately.

### Step 2: Provide a balanced, age-appropriate diet

Children need a balanced diet that provides protein, calcium, vitamin D, and other essential nutrients.

Examples include:
- Breast milk or age-appropriate infant formula for young babies.
- Milk and dairy products suitable for the child's age.
- Fish, eggs, meat, and other protein-rich foods.
- Vegetables and a variety of foods during complementary feeding.

Do not give high-dose calcium, vitamin D, or other supplements simply because your child has not developed teeth. Supplementation should be based on the child's needs and guidance from a healthcare professional.

### Step 3: Arrange a dental examination when needed

If your child has no teeth by 18 months, arrange an appointment with a pediatric dentist.

The dentist may:
- Examine the gums, jaw, and mouth.
- Assess tooth development.
- Ask about family history, premature birth, and general health.
- Recommend further investigations if an abnormality is suspected.

Not every child with late teething needs an X-ray or laboratory tests. The dentist will decide based on the examination.

### Step 4: Do not try to force teeth to erupt

Parents should avoid:
- Poking, scraping, or cutting the gums to make teeth appear faster.
- Giving babies hard, sharp, or unhygienic objects to chew.
- Using teething medicines or gels without professional guidance.
- Giving high-dose calcium or vitamins without medical advice.

These methods do not make teeth erupt faster and may injure the gums or harm the child.

## 7. How to care for a baby during teething

Parents can help relieve teething discomfort with safe methods:

- **Massage the gums:** Wash your hands and gently rub the uncomfortable gum area with a clean finger.
- **Offer a safe teething ring:** Choose an age-appropriate product, clean it regularly, and supervise your baby.
- **Cool the teething ring:** A teething ring can be chilled in the refrigerator, but do not freeze it. Extremely cold or hard objects may injure the gums.
- **Wipe away drool:** Use a soft, clean cloth to gently wipe the mouth and chin to reduce skin irritation.
- **Continue feeding:** Maintain age-appropriate feeding. If your baby has started solids, soft and cool foods may be more comfortable.

Avoid amber teething necklaces and teething gels containing benzocaine or lidocaine unless specifically advised by a qualified healthcare professional. These products can be dangerous, especially for young children.

## 8. When should you take your child to a doctor or dentist?

Arrange an appointment with a pediatric dentist or pediatrician if:

- Your child has no primary teeth by 18 months.
- Your child has unusual changes in the gums, jaw, or face.
- Teeth appear misaligned or seem unable to erupt.
- Delayed teething occurs alongside poor growth or other developmental concerns.
- Your child has significant pain, severe gum swelling, pus, or fever.

If your child has a high fever, unusual lethargy, refuses feeds, has difficulty breathing, or shows signs of dehydration, seek medical care promptly. Do not assume these symptoms are simply caused by teething.

## 9. When should your child have their first dental visit?

Schedule your child's first dental appointment when the first tooth appears, and no later than their first birthday.

This visit allows the dentist to:
- Examine the teeth and gums.
- Monitor tooth eruption and development.
- Show parents how to clean their child's teeth.
- Provide advice on preventing tooth decay and caring for baby teeth.

As soon as the first tooth appears, begin brushing with a soft, age-appropriate toothbrush and a smear of fluoride toothpaste about the size of a grain of rice. An adult should help with brushing.

## 10. Conclusion

Most babies start teething between six and twelve months and have all 20 primary teeth by around three years of age. However, eruption timing can vary considerably from child to child.

A baby who has no teeth at six to twelve months does not necessarily have a problem. If a child still has no teeth by 18 months, parents should arrange a dental examination to identify possible causes and receive appropriate guidance.

Avoid giving calcium or vitamin supplements without advice or trying methods to force teeth to erupt. Monitor your child's overall development, provide balanced nutrition, and begin good oral hygiene as soon as the first tooth appears.
