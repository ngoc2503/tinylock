Many parents experience a situation in which their child prefers drinking milk and refuses rice, porridge, vegetables, meat, or other foods. Some children turn away as soon as they see a plate of food. Others eat only a few spoonfuls before asking for milk, or cry when their parents do not give them another bottle.

When this continues, parents may worry about nutritional deficiencies, poor weight gain, or developmental problems. In fact, the reasons may include excessive milk consumption, established feeding habits, developmental stages, difficulties with chewing and swallowing, or underlying health conditions.

So, what should you do if your child only wants milk and refuses food? The first steps are to identify the cause, adjust the feeding routine according to your child's age, and help them become familiar with different foods patiently and without pressure.

## 1. Why does my child only want milk and refuse food?

### 1.1. Your child drinks too much milk and is not hungry at mealtimes

This is a common reason among children older than 12 months.

Milk provides energy and makes children feel full. If your child drinks a large amount of milk before meals, drinks it frequently throughout the day, or receives milk immediately whenever they refuse food, they may not feel hungry enough to eat solid foods.

A cycle can gradually develop:

1. Your child drinks a lot of milk and does not feel hungry.
2. You worry that your child is not eating enough, so you offer more milk.
3. Your child has fewer opportunities to become familiar with solid foods.
4. Your child gradually develops a preference for drinking milk instead of eating.

Over time, a diet that relies too heavily on milk may lack nutritional variety, particularly iron.

### 1.2. Your child is used to liquid, soft, and easy-to-swallow foods

Some children become accustomed to drinking milk or eating smooth purees for a long time and may resist foods with thicker or more varied textures.

When offered rice, meat, vegetables, or foods that require chewing, they may:

- Hold food in their mouth without chewing.
- Spit food out.
- Gag when they encounter lumps or different textures.
- Accept only smooth purees or foods with a particular consistency.

Children need time to learn how to chew, move food around in their mouths, and coordinate swallowing. However, if your child frequently coughs, chokes, or struggles to swallow, seek a professional assessment rather than assuming they are simply picky eaters.

### 1.3. Your child may be going through a picky-eating phase

As children grow, they begin developing their own preferences and wanting more control over what they eat. Some may refuse unfamiliar foods, prefer only a few familiar dishes, or change their food preferences from time to time.

This can be a common part of development in young children. They may need to encounter a food repeatedly before they are willing to taste it.

If parents frequently force children to eat, scold them, or immediately replace refused food with milk, the problem may become harder to resolve.

### 1.4. Your child may dislike the taste, smell, or texture of certain foods

Children differ in how sensitive they are to the smell, taste, color, and texture of food.

Some children dislike strong-smelling vegetables, chewy meat, lumpy foods, or dishes containing several ingredients mixed together. Others prefer foods to be served separately rather than combined.

This does not necessarily mean your child has a nutritional deficiency or a medical condition. You may need to adjust food preparation, portion sizes, or presentation to make foods easier for your child to accept.

### 1.5. Your child may be teething or experiencing a health problem

Some conditions can make eating solid foods uncomfortable:

- Teething, sore gums, or mouth pain.
- Mouth ulcers, sore throat, or oral infections.
- Nasal congestion that makes it harder to coordinate breathing and chewing.
- Constipation, bloating, or abdominal pain.
- Reflux that causes discomfort during or after meals.

If your child previously ate normally but suddenly wants only milk, observe other symptoms and consider arranging a medical assessment.

### 1.6. Your child may have feeding or sensory difficulties

Some children have difficulty chewing, managing food in their mouths, or coordinating swallowing. Others may be unusually sensitive to the smell, taste, or sensation of certain foods.

Watch for signs such as:

- Frequent coughing or choking while eating or drinking.
- Food regularly spilling out of the mouth.
- Acceptance of only a very limited range of food textures.
- Gagging or vomiting when seeing, smelling, or tasting unfamiliar foods.
- Unusually long mealtimes because chewing or swallowing is difficult.

In these situations, your child may need an assessment by a doctor and support from a nutrition or feeding and swallowing specialist.

## 2. Can drinking too much milk affect a child's health?

Milk is an important source of nutrition, but it should not completely replace a varied diet once a child reaches the appropriate age for complementary foods.

If a child older than 12 months drinks too much milk, several problems may occur.

**Reduced appetite:** Milk can make children feel full, leaving them less interested in rice, vegetables, meat, fish, and other foods.

**Risk of iron deficiency:** Cow's milk contains very little iron. Drinking too much may reduce a child's intake of iron-rich foods, increasing the risk of iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anemia.

**Limited nutritional variety:** A milk-heavy diet may lack fiber and certain vitamins and minerals found in vegetables, fruits, grains, meat, fish, and legumes.

**Dental problems:** If children frequently fall asleep with a milk bottle or drink milk repeatedly throughout the night, their teeth may be exposed to sugars in milk for extended periods, increasing the risk of tooth decay.

### How much milk should a child older than one year drink?

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) generally recommends around 16–24 ounces (473–750 mL) of milk per day after a child's first birthday, depending on their needs and overall diet. Drinking more than this may reduce their appetite for iron-rich foods.

This is a general reference range, not a strict target for every child. Actual needs depend on age, growth, the type of milk, and other sources of nutrition.

**Important age-related guidance:**

- **Younger than 6 months:** Breast milk or appropriate infant formula is the main source of nutrition. Complementary foods should not be introduced yet.
- **6–11 months:** Breast milk or infant formula remains the main source of nutrition, but complementary foods should be introduced at around 6 months. Cow's milk should not be used as the main drink instead of breast milk or infant formula before 12 months.
- **12 months and older:** Children should gradually eat a wider variety of foods. Pasteurized whole cow's milk may be included if appropriate for their age and health, but it should not replace meals.

## 3. What should you do if your child only wants milk and refuses food?

### Step 1: Adjust the amount and timing of milk

If your child is older than 12 months and drinks too much milk, gradually adjust the amount rather than stopping it suddenly.

- Keep a record of how much milk your child drinks each day.
- Avoid giving milk immediately before main meals.
- Offer solid foods first, then provide milk at an appropriate time.
- Offer plain water between meals instead of giving milk continuously.
- Gradually reduce unnecessary milk feeds, especially those given out of habit rather than genuine hunger.

Do not apply this milk-reduction approach to children younger than 12 months. Breast milk or infant formula remains an essential source of nutrition at this age.

### Step 2: Establish a regular eating schedule

Children benefit from a reasonably consistent routine that helps them develop natural hunger and fullness cues.

You can consider the following general routine:

- Three main meals each day for children who are ready to eat regular meals.
- One or two nutritious snacks, depending on age and needs.
- Reasonable intervals between meals and snacks.
- Avoid continuous milk drinking, biscuits, and snacks between meals.

For infants aged 6–8 months, WHO recommends starting with 2–3 complementary meals per day. Children aged 9–23 months generally need 3–4 complementary meals per day, with 1–2 additional suitable snacks.

The schedule should be adjusted to each child's age, feeding skills, and individual needs.

### Step 3: Start with small amounts of food

If your child is used to drinking milk and often refuses food, do not begin by serving a full bowl of rice or porridge.

Instead:

- Offer a small amount, such as a few spoonfuls or age-appropriate pieces.
- Serve one familiar food alongside one new food.
- Gradually increase portions as your child becomes more comfortable.
- Allow your child to hold, explore, and taste food safely.

The initial goal is not to make your child eat a large amount. It is to help them become familiar with different smells, tastes, and textures.

### Step 4: Offer a variety of foods and preparation methods

Rather than focusing only on rice or porridge, introduce different food groups appropriate for your child's age:

- **Iron- and protein-rich foods:** Beef, chicken, fish, eggs, tofu, and legumes.
- **Vegetables:** Pumpkin, carrots, broccoli, leafy greens, and other suitable vegetables.
- **Fruits:** Bananas, avocados, papayas, oranges, and soft fruits.
- **Grains and starchy foods:** Rice, sweet potatoes, oats, noodles, and suitable grains.

Try different preparation methods, such as steaming until soft, boiling, mashing, chopping into suitable pieces, or serving foods separately.

Avoid keeping your child on smooth purees for too long if they are developmentally ready to explore a wider range of textures.

### Step 5: Offer new foods repeatedly

A child may need to encounter a new food several times before accepting it.

Parents can:

- Introduce a small amount of the new food.
- Allow the child to look at, touch, smell, and taste it at their own pace.
- Combine the new food with something familiar.
- Try different preparation methods without creating pressure.
- Offer the food again after a few days if the child was not ready to try it.

According to the CDC, young children may need around 8–10 exposures to a food before they are willing to try it. Do not force your child to finish a new food the first time it is offered.

### Step 6: Let your child participate in mealtimes

When developmentally appropriate, allow your child to hold a spoon, pick up food, or choose between two healthy options.

You can:

- Let your child sit and eat with the family.
- Model healthy eating by enjoying a variety of foods yourself.
- Offer a choice between two vegetables or fruits.
- Praise your child's effort when they try something new.
- Keep mealtimes pleasant rather than turning them into a struggle.

Parents decide what foods are offered and when meals take place. Children should be encouraged to decide how much to eat within the limits of their individual needs.

### Step 7: Limit screen use during meals

Avoid letting your child eat while watching a phone, tablet, or television.

When children are focused on screens, they may pay less attention to hunger, fullness, chewing, and swallowing. Parents may also find it harder to recognize whether the child actually wants to eat.

Create a calm mealtime environment where an adult can observe and support the child when needed.

## 4. Sample meal ideas to help children become familiar with food

Meals should be adjusted according to age, chewing and swallowing skills, allergies, and nutritional needs. The following are general ideas:

| Meal | Suggested foods |
|---|---|
| Breakfast | Minced meat and pumpkin porridge, or oatmeal with soft fruit |
| Morning snack | Mashed banana, avocado, or age-appropriate yogurt |
| Lunch | Soft rice, steamed fish, and cooked vegetables |
| Afternoon snack | Soft fruit or a suitable snack |
| Dinner | Chicken and vegetable porridge, or soft rice with egg and vegetables |

For young children, adjust the texture, softness, and size of food to match their feeding skills. Always supervise children while eating and avoid choking hazards such as whole nuts, whole grapes, hard raw carrot pieces, or tough, round pieces of food.

Do not use this sample menu as a fixed meal plan for every age group.

## 5. When should you take your child to a doctor?

If your child has relied mainly on milk for an extended period, discuss their diet, growth, and risk of nutrient deficiencies with a pediatrician or qualified nutrition professional.

Seek medical advice, especially if your child has any of the following signs:

- Poor weight gain, weight loss, or a downward growth trend.
- Tiredness, paleness, reduced activity, or frequent sleepiness.
- Eating very few food groups and relying almost entirely on milk.
- Frequent coughing, choking, gagging, or vomiting during meals.
- Difficulty chewing or swallowing, or acceptance of only a very limited range of textures.
- Recurrent abdominal pain, constipation, vomiting, or discomfort during meals.
- Delayed development of feeding skills or loss of previously acquired eating skills.

If your child has difficulty breathing, turns blue, chokes on food, or cannot swallow, seek emergency care immediately.

A doctor may assess growth, dietary intake, and order tests for iron deficiency or other conditions if necessary. Do not give high-dose iron supplements or vitamins without professional guidance.

## 6. Common mistakes parents should avoid

**Forcing children to eat:** Scolding, threatening, or physically restraining children to feed them may make them fearful of mealtimes and increase food refusal.

**Offering milk immediately after food refusal:** If every refusal is followed by milk, children may learn to wait for milk instead of exploring solid foods.

**Stopping milk suddenly:** This is particularly inappropriate for children younger than 12 months, as it may affect their main source of nutrition. For older children, gradually adjust milk intake while ensuring that their diet remains nutritionally adequate.

**Offering only a few favorite foods:** Children need opportunities to try different food groups to develop varied preferences and obtain essential nutrients.

**Using appetite stimulants or supplements without advice:** These products do not address the underlying reason for food refusal and may not be necessary.

**Comparing your child with other children:** Every child has their own growth pattern, food preferences, and nutritional needs. Monitor your child's individual growth over time.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

**Can my child get enough nutrients by drinking milk instead of eating meals?**

Milk should not be considered a complete replacement for varied meals, especially after a child is older than 6 months and has started complementary feeding. Milk alone does not provide the full range of iron, fiber, and nutrients found in different food groups.

**Should I stop giving milk completely so my child will eat solid foods?**

No. Children younger than 12 months still need breast milk or infant formula as their main source of nutrition. For children older than one year, adjust the amount and timing of milk rather than eliminating it completely if it remains part of their diet.

**Can drinking only milk cause anemia?**

Not every child who drinks a lot of milk develops anemia. However, excessive milk intake combined with low consumption of iron-rich foods can increase the risk of iron deficiency. A doctor can assess your child and recommend testing if there are risk factors or concerning symptoms.

**What should I do if my child refuses vegetables and only wants milk?**

Offer small amounts of vegetables, try different preparation methods, and serve them alongside familiar foods. Continue offering them repeatedly without pressure, and set a good example by eating vegetables together.

**How long does it take for a child to stop relying only on milk?**

There is no fixed timeline. Some children improve after their milk intake and meal routine are adjusted. Others may need longer professional support if they have sensory, chewing, swallowing, or health-related difficulties.

## Conclusion

A child who only wants milk and refuses food may be drinking too much milk, struggling with different food textures, going through a picky-eating phase, or experiencing difficulties with chewing and swallowing. Parents should adjust milk intake according to age, establish regular mealtimes, offer a variety of foods, and encourage exploration without force.

If your child has poor growth, looks pale, frequently coughs or chokes while eating, or accepts only a very limited range of foods, seek medical advice for an early assessment.
