Communication is an essential skill that helps children express their thoughts, share feelings, make friends, and build relationships. A child who communicates well does not necessarily need to speak a lot or fluently. They should be able to express what they want, listen to others, ask questions, and respond appropriately in different situations.

However, not every child feels confident when communicating. Some children are shy around strangers, some frequently interrupt others, while others struggle to start conversations or explain their thoughts.

So, how can parents help their children become better communicators? The key is to create a positive communication environment, talk regularly, and give children opportunities to practice in everyday situations.

## 1. Why are communication skills important for children?

Communication does more than help children talk to other people. It also supports many areas of their development.

**Helps children express needs and emotions:** Children who can communicate their needs can explain what they want, whether they feel happy or sad, and when they need help, rather than relying only on crying, shouting, or challenging behavior.

**Develops language and thinking skills:** Through conversations, questions, and explanations, children expand their vocabulary, organize their ideas, and learn to express themselves clearly.

**Builds friendships and relationships:** Greeting others, listening, sharing, and taking turns help children connect with friends, teachers, and people around them.

**Builds confidence:** When children can share their opinions and feel that others listen to them, they gradually become more confident in group activities.

**Teaches conflict resolution:** Good communication helps children negotiate, explain their viewpoints, apologize, and resolve disagreements without hurting others.

## 2. Signs that your child may need more communication practice

Every child has a different personality and developmental pace. However, parents may want to pay attention if their child regularly:

- Feels shy about starting conversations or joining discussions.
- Struggles to express needs, thoughts, or feelings.
- Frequently interrupts, speaks too loudly, or has difficulty waiting for their turn.
- Pays little attention to what others are saying.
- Finds it difficult to maintain a conversation or usually gives very short answers.
- Does not know how to ask questions to get more information.
- Struggles to share, negotiate, or resolve disagreements with friends.
- Becomes upset or withdraws when others do not understand them.

These behaviors do not necessarily indicate a developmental problem. Some children simply need more time, encouragement, or opportunities to feel comfortable communicating.

## 3. How can parents teach their children to communicate better?

### Method 1: Be a positive communication role model

Children learn communication not only through instruction but also by observing adults.

Parents should:
- Speak politely and calmly with other people.
- Use words such as "thank you," "sorry," "please," and "excuse me."
- Avoid shouting, mocking, or insulting others during disagreements.
- Listen without interrupting when someone else is speaking.
- Admit mistakes and apologize when they behave inappropriately.

For example, if you accidentally drop your child's toy, you can say: "I'm sorry I dropped your toy. I'll pick it up for you."

Through everyday situations like this, children learn that polite communication and taking responsibility for mistakes are normal parts of life.

### Method 2: Spend time talking with your child every day

To help children communicate well, parents need to create regular opportunities for them to speak and be heard.

Try spending 10–15 minutes each day talking with your child without distractions from phones or television.

Start with simple questions:
- "What did you do today?"
- "What made you happiest today?"
- "Which game do you like most? Why?"
- "If you could change one thing about today, what would it be?"

Avoid turning conversations into interviews. Share your own experiences too, so your child feels that communication is a two-way exchange.

### Method 3: Teach your child active listening

Good communication is not just about speaking. It also involves listening.

Parents can teach children to:
- Pay attention to the person who is speaking.
- Wait until the other person finishes before responding.
- Avoid interrupting.
- Ask for clarification when they do not understand.
- Repeat the main idea to confirm that they have understood correctly.

For example, when your child talks about playing with a friend, you might respond: "So today you and your friend built a tower together, and then it fell down. Is that right?"

This response shows your child that you are listening and teaches them how to receive and confirm information.

Note: Do not force children to maintain constant direct eye contact. They can show attention by turning toward the speaker, responding verbally, or using gestures in ways that suit their abilities and comfort.

### Method 4: Encourage your child to express thoughts and feelings

Some children speak very little because they do not know how to express what they are thinking or feeling.

Parents can help expand their communication by:
- Introducing words for emotions such as happy, sad, disappointed, worried, and angry.
- Encouraging children to explain their reasons rather than only answering "yes" or "no."
- Offering choices when children do not know what to say.
- Avoiding criticism or ridicule when their words are unclear.

For example:

Child: "I don't like my friend."

Parent: "Are you sad because your friend didn't let you play? Would you like to tell me what happened?"

As children become more comfortable expressing themselves, encourage them to find their own words and explain their thoughts.

### Method 5: Read books and tell stories together

Reading is a useful activity for developing vocabulary, listening comprehension, and storytelling skills.

After reading a story, parents can ask:
- "What is the character doing?"
- "Why do you think they are sad?"
- "What would you do if you were in that situation?"
- "What do you think will happen next?"

For young children, use picture books, point to images, and name familiar objects, animals, and actions.

For older children, encourage them to retell stories in order, including the beginning, middle, and ending.

Children do not need to give perfect answers. Allow them time to think and express themselves in their own way.

### Method 6: Create opportunities to communicate through play

Play provides a natural environment for children to practice communication without feeling tested.

Parents can try:

- **Role-play:** Play shopkeeper, doctor, teacher, or restaurant worker to practice greetings, asking questions, and answering.
- **Guessing games:** One person describes an object while the other tries to guess what it is.
- **Story building:** Take turns adding one sentence to create a story.
- **Turn-taking games:** Play board games, building games, or group activities to practice waiting and communicating.
- **Problem-solving role-play:** Practice asking to join a game, requesting help, or resolving disagreements.

Parents should join in, encourage children to take the lead, and avoid correcting every mistake, which may reduce their enjoyment.

### Method 7: Teach your child how to start and maintain conversations

Some children can answer questions but do not know how to start a conversation or keep it going.

Parents can teach simple phrases:

**To start a conversation:**
- "Hi, can I play with you?"
- "What game are you playing?"
- "My name is ... What's yours?"

**To keep a conversation going:**
- "What happened next?"
- "What do you like most?"
- "Why do you think that?"
- "Something similar happened to me."

**To end a conversation politely:**
- "I have to go now. See you later!"
- "Thanks for talking with me."

Parents can practice these situations through role-play before children encounter them in real life.

### Method 8: Teach polite and respectful communication

Children need to understand that good communication is not only about saying the right words. It is also about respecting the person they are speaking with.

Teach your child to:
- Greet familiar people.
- Say "please" when asking for help.
- Say "thank you" when someone helps them.
- Apologize when they hurt someone.
- Avoid mocking other people's appearance, voices, or differences.
- Avoid sharing other people's private information without permission.
- Respect another person's choice not to hug, hold hands, or have physical contact.

Explain the reasons behind these behaviors rather than simply asking children to memorize polite phrases.

### Method 9: Encourage participation in group activities

Group activities allow children to practice communication in different situations.

Parents can encourage children to participate in:
- Games with friends.
- Sports or clubs.
- Art, music, and craft activities.
- Group activities at school.
- Family gatherings or playtime with relatives.

Shy children may initially prefer to observe or participate for only a short time. Do not force them to speak a lot immediately. Create opportunities gradually and acknowledge small improvements.

### Method 10: Praise your child's effort and progress

When children initiate greetings, listen attentively, ask questions, or express feelings appropriately, acknowledge their efforts specifically.

For example:
- "I'm happy that you greeted your friend today."
- "You waited until your friend finished speaking before answering. That was very polite."
- "You explained what you wanted very clearly."

Do not praise children only when they speak fluently or talk a lot. Recognize small efforts too, especially when a child is shy or needs more time to communicate.

## 4. Communication activities for different age groups

Communication skills develop gradually. Parents should choose activities that match their child's abilities.

**Children aged 0–12 months:**
- Talk, sing, and read picture books to your baby regularly.
- Respond to your baby's babbling, sounds, and gestures.
- Imitate your baby's sounds, then pause to allow a response.
- Name familiar people, objects, and actions.
- Combine words with facial expressions and gestures.

**Children aged 1–3 years:**
- Name objects, actions, and emotions.
- Offer simple choices to help children express their preferences.
- Read picture books and encourage children to point to and name pictures.
- Practice short phrases such as "I want water."
- Play pretend and imitation games.

**Children aged 3–5 years:**
- Encourage children to talk about what happened during the day.
- Ask open-ended questions that invite explanations.
- Practice greetings, saying thank you, and apologizing.
- Play storytelling, guessing, and role-play games.
- Teach children to take turns and share during play.

**Children aged 6–8 years:**
- Encourage children to express opinions and explain their reasons.
- Practice listening and responding during discussions.
- Read stories and discuss the characters' actions.
- Practice resolving conflicts through conversation.
- Encourage participation in group activities.

**Children aged 9–11 years:**
- Practice presenting opinions with reasons and examples.
- Discuss topics that interest the child.
- Encourage asking questions and expressing disagreement respectfully.
- Practice communication in presentations, teamwork, and conflict resolution.
- Teach responsible online communication, including protecting personal information and avoiding hurtful comments.

## 5. Parenting mistakes to avoid when teaching communication

- **Speaking for the child in every situation:** When children are able to answer for themselves, give them the opportunity.
- **Forcing children to speak in front of groups:** This may increase anxiety and make them more afraid of communicating.
- **Mocking or comparing children:** Avoid telling children they communicate less effectively than their friends or siblings.
- **Constantly interrupting to correct mistakes:** Focus first on what the child is trying to communicate, then naturally model the correct expression.
- **Teaching children to speak without teaching them to listen:** Communication is a two-way process.
- **Asking too many questions in a row:** This can make children feel as though they are being tested rather than having a conversation.
- **Forcing children to make direct eye contact:** Assess attention and interaction flexibly, while respecting the child's comfort.

## 6. When should parents seek professional support?

Children develop at different rates. However, parents should speak with a pediatrician or speech-language pathologist if they notice signs such as:

- Little or no response to sounds or other people's voices.
- Apparent difficulty hearing or understanding simple instructions.
- Delays in age-appropriate language and communication milestones.
- Difficulty understanding gestures, facial expressions, or other people's communicative intentions.
- Significant difficulty with turn-taking, joint attention, or social interaction.
- Loss of communication or language skills the child previously had.
- Communication difficulties that significantly affect learning, daily activities, or relationships.

In particular, if a child loses language or communication skills they previously had, parents should arrange an assessment promptly rather than waiting for the child to improve on their own.

A professional may assess hearing, language comprehension, expression, speech, and social interaction to determine what kind of support the child needs.

Note: Being shy, speaking less, or not enjoying eye contact does not automatically mean that a child has a developmental disorder. Assessment should consider each child's overall abilities and individual characteristics.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

**Does a child who speaks very little have poor communication skills?**

Not necessarily. Some children are introverted, prefer observing, or need more time to feel comfortable. Good communication does not mean talking a lot. Parents should consider the child's understanding, expression, interaction, and progress.

**Should I enroll my child in communication skills classes?**

Suitable classes can provide additional opportunities to practice, especially through group activities and role-play. However, communication should also be practiced regularly at home and in everyday life. Classes do not replace professional assessment when a child has developmental difficulties.

**How much time should parents spend talking with their children each day?**

There is no fixed amount of time for every family. Parents can start with 10–15 minutes of focused conversation each day and use activities such as meals, walks, reading, and playtime to create additional opportunities for communication.

**Should I correct my child immediately when they say something incorrectly?**

Avoid constantly interrupting or making your child feel embarrassed. You can repeat the child's sentence using the correct and natural expression. For example, if the child uses incorrect grammar, respond with a complete and correctly structured sentence instead of criticizing the mistake.

## Conclusion

To help children communicate better, parents should create a safe, positive environment with plenty of opportunities for interaction. Be a good role model by speaking politely, listening to your child, reading books, playing together, and encouraging children to express their thoughts.

Most importantly, do not pressure children to talk more or communicate in exactly the same way as other children. Respect their personality, abilities, and developmental pace, and seek professional support when you notice signs of concern.
