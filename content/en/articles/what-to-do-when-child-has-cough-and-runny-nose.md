Coughing and a runny nose are common symptoms in children, especially when they catch a cold or develop a respiratory infection. Most cases improve with home care, but some children may develop bronchiolitis, pneumonia, or breathing difficulties that require medical attention.

So, what should parents do when a child has a cough and runny nose? It is important to assess the severity of the symptoms, keep the nose clear, provide enough fluids, and recognize warning signs that require medical care.

## 1. Why do children develop a cough and runny nose?

Coughing and a runny nose can occur together for several reasons.

**Common cold:** This is one of the most common causes. Children may sneeze, have a runny or blocked nose, develop a sore throat, cough, and sometimes have a mild fever. Most symptoms improve within one to two weeks.

**Flu and other respiratory viruses:** Children may develop fever, cough, a runny nose, sore throat, fatigue, and reduced appetite. Influenza and COVID-19 can cause symptoms similar to those of a common cold.

**Bronchiolitis:** This condition is common in children under 2 years, especially infants. It may begin with a runny nose and mild cough, followed by rapid breathing, wheezing, or difficulty breathing. Symptoms can worsen during the first few days.

**Pneumonia:** Possible symptoms include cough, fever, rapid breathing, difficulty breathing, chest pain, poor feeding, or unusual tiredness. Young children may not show obvious symptoms, so parents should pay attention to breathing and general behavior.

**Respiratory allergies:** Children may sneeze frequently and have a clear runny nose, itchy nose, or itchy eyes, usually without a fever. Symptoms may persist or recur after exposure to dust, pollen, animal dander, or other allergens.

**Environmental irritants:** Cigarette smoke, dust, air pollution, chemical odors, and very dry air can irritate the respiratory tract and worsen coughing.

Parents should not assume that yellow or green nasal mucus automatically means a bacterial infection. Mucus can change from clear to yellow or green during a viral cold.

## 2. What should you do if your child has a cough and runny nose?

If your child is alert, breathing comfortably, feeding or drinking normally, and has no warning signs, you can provide the following care at home.

### Step 1: Clean the nose with saline

A blocked nose can make it difficult for children to breathe through their nose, feed, and sleep. Nasal mucus dripping down the throat may also trigger coughing.

Parents can:
- Use age-appropriate saline drops or spray, such as 0.9% sodium chloride.
- Apply an appropriate amount according to the product instructions to soften nasal mucus.
- For babies who cannot blow their noses, gently use an infant nasal suction device when needed.
- For older children, teach them to blow their noses gently, one nostril at a time.
- Clean the nose before feeding if congestion makes it difficult for the child to feed.

Do not suction too forcefully or too frequently, as this may irritate or injure the nasal lining. Do not use medicated decongestant nasal sprays unless a healthcare professional has advised you to do so.

### Step 2: Offer enough fluids and continue feeding

When children have a cold, they may eat or drink less. Fever, rapid breathing, and nasal discharge can also increase the risk of dehydration.

Parents should:
- Continue breastfeeding on demand.
- Continue formula feeding using the usual safe preparation method.
- Offer water regularly to children who are already drinking water and eating a varied diet.
- Offer soft foods such as soup or porridge if the child does not feel like eating much.
- Avoid forcing food, but monitor fluid intake and urination.

Seek medical advice if your child feeds significantly less than usual, cannot drink, vomits repeatedly, or urinates less often.

### Step 3: Soothe the cough

Coughing is a natural reflex that helps clear mucus and irritants from the airways. It is not always necessary or helpful to suppress every cough.

Parents can:
- Offer enough fluids to keep the throat moist.
- For children older than 1 year, consider a small amount of honey to soothe a cough, such as 2–5 mL when needed.
- Encourage rest and avoid strenuous activity while the child is unwell.
- Keep the child away from cigarette smoke, dust, perfume, and chemical odors.
- If the indoor air is dry, use a clean cool-mist humidifier and maintain it according to the manufacturer's instructions.

**Never give honey to a child under 1 year of age** because of the risk of infant botulism.

Do not let children inhale steam directly from a pot or kettle of hot water because this can cause serious burns.

### Step 4: Help your child rest and sleep comfortably

Coughing and nasal congestion may interrupt sleep, especially at night.

Parents can:
- Keep the bedroom clean, ventilated, and at a comfortable temperature.
- Gently clean the nose before bedtime if the child is congested.
- Allow extra rest and reduce activities that may cause fatigue.
- Keep the child away from cigarette smoke and other respiratory irritants.

For infants, always place the baby on their back on a firm, flat sleep surface. Do not use pillows or rolled towels to elevate the baby's head during sleep. Do not change safe-sleep practices because the baby has a stuffy nose.

### Step 5: Monitor temperature and breathing

Observe your child throughout the day, especially if they are very young.

Pay attention to:
- Whether your child has a fever and how high it is.
- Whether breathing is faster than usual.
- Whether the chest or area below the ribs pulls inward during inhalation.
- Whether your child has wheezing, noisy breathing, or blue lips.
- Whether your child is feeding, drinking, and urinating normally.
- Whether your child is alert, playing, and responding as usual.

If your child has difficulty breathing, is struggling to breathe, or develops bluish skin, seek emergency medical care immediately.

## 3. How to care for children with coughs and runny noses by age

**Children under 3 months**

Newborns have small airways and limited ability to fight infections. Contact a healthcare professional early if your baby develops a cough or runny nose, especially if feeding becomes difficult or other symptoms appear.

A temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher in a baby under 3 months requires urgent medical assessment. Do not give cough medicine, cold medicine, or medicated nasal sprays without medical advice.

**Children aged 3–12 months**

- Continue breastfeeding or appropriate formula feeding.
- Use saline drops and gentle nasal suction when needed.
- Clean the nose before feeding if congestion makes feeding difficult.
- Monitor milk intake, wet diapers, and breathing.
- Do not give honey, cough medicine, or over-the-counter cold medicine unless advised by a healthcare professional.

**Children aged 1–5 years**

- Encourage regular fluid intake.
- Use saline drops or spray to help clear the nose.
- Teach your child to blow their nose gently.
- Honey may be used to soothe a cough because the child is older than 1 year.
- Encourage rest and offer soft, easy-to-swallow foods.
- Do not give cough syrup or combination cold medicine without professional advice.

**Children aged 6–11 years**

- Teach children to clean their noses and blow gently.
- Encourage adequate fluids and sleep.
- Provide balanced meals and rest when they feel unwell.
- Teach them to cover their mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing and to wash their hands regularly.
- Use medicines only when appropriate for the child's age and according to professional advice or the product label.

## 4. Does a child with a cough and runny nose need medicine?

Not every child with a cough and runny nose needs medication. Most common colds are caused by viruses, so antibiotics do not treat them.

Parents should remember:

**Cough and cold medicines:** Do not give young children cough syrups, combination cold medicines, or decongestants without professional advice. These products may contain several active ingredients and can cause side effects or accidental overdoses. Many guidelines do not recommend over-the-counter cough and cold medicines for children under 6 unless directed by a healthcare professional.

**Fever medicine:** If your child has a fever and is uncomfortable, a doctor or pharmacist may advise paracetamol (acetaminophen) or ibuprofen based on the child's age, weight, and health. Do not estimate the dose, give aspirin to children, or alternate fever medicines without medical advice.

**Antibiotics:** Do not use antibiotics simply because your child coughs frequently or has yellow or green nasal mucus. Antibiotics only work against certain bacterial infections and must be prescribed when appropriate.

**Nasal sprays:** Saline drops or sprays may help clean the nose and loosen mucus. Medicated decongestant sprays require professional advice about age, dosage, and duration.

Never give children adult medicines, reuse old prescriptions, or combine several cold medicines without checking their ingredients.

## 5. When should you take your child to a doctor?

Arrange a medical assessment if your child has any of the following:

- A cough lasting longer than 3 weeks or not showing signs of improvement.
- Symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement.
- A fever lasting more than 4 days or returning after the child initially improves.
- A worsening cough, wheezing, or chest pain.
- Ear pain, persistent crying, or fluid draining from the ear.
- Significantly reduced feeding, eating, or drinking.
- Reduced urination, dry lips, fewer tears, or other signs of dehydration.
- An underlying condition such as asthma, heart disease, lung disease, or a weakened immune system.
- Unusual tiredness or symptoms that are getting worse.

Infants under 3 months with a cough or runny nose should receive early medical advice. A temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher requires urgent assessment.

## 6. Warning signs that require emergency care

Do not wait for your child to recover at home if any of the following occur:

- Difficulty breathing, very rapid breathing, or obvious effort when breathing.
- Deep inward pulling of the chest or area below the ribs with each breath.
- Blue lips, tongue, or face.
- Pauses in breathing, abnormal breathing, or noisy breathing while resting.
- Inability to breastfeed, drink, or swallow because of breathing difficulty.
- Unusual drowsiness, difficulty waking, seizures, or poor responsiveness.
- Severe dehydration, such as no urination for a prolonged period, a very dry mouth, or no tears when crying.

**Call your local emergency number or take your child to the nearest emergency department immediately.** Do not try to give food, drinks, or oral medicine while your child is struggling to breathe.

## 7. How to help prevent coughs and runny noses

Parents can reduce the risk of respiratory infections by encouraging these habits:

- Wash hands with soap before feeding or caring for children and after cleaning their noses.
- Teach children to cover their mouth and nose with a tissue or their elbow when coughing or sneezing.
- Clean toys, frequently touched surfaces, and shared living spaces.
- Avoid close contact with people who have respiratory infection symptoms.
- Do not smoke inside the home, in the car, or near children.
- Keep indoor spaces ventilated and reduce dust and other irritants.
- Provide balanced nutrition, adequate sleep, and age-appropriate physical activity.
- Keep vaccinations up to date, including recommended vaccines that protect against respiratory illnesses.

## 8. Frequently asked questions

**Does a child with a cough and runny nose need antibiotics?**

Usually not. Most common colds are caused by viruses and improve on their own. Antibiotics do not kill viruses and should only be used when a doctor identifies a bacterial infection that requires treatment.

**Does green nasal mucus mean my child has a bacterial infection?**

Not necessarily. During a viral cold, nasal mucus may change from clear to yellow or green. Mucus color alone is not enough to determine whether antibiotics are needed.

**What should I do if my child coughs frequently at night?**

Gently clean the nose before bedtime, offer enough fluids, and keep the bedroom comfortable and ventilated. For children older than 1 year, a small amount of honey may help soothe a cough. If the cough is severe, accompanied by breathing difficulties or wheezing, or repeatedly occurs at night over a long period, arrange a medical assessment.

**How long does a child's cough and runny nose usually last?**

A common cold usually improves within one to two weeks. However, a cough may continue for some time after other symptoms have improved. Seek medical advice if the cough lasts longer than 3 weeks, symptoms worsen, or other concerning signs develop.

**Can I bathe my child when they have a cough and runny nose?**

Yes, if your child is alert and not excessively tired. Keep the bath brief, use comfortably warm water, dry your child afterward, and dress them appropriately. There is no need to avoid bathing completely just because your child has a cold.

## Conclusion

When your child has a cough and runny nose, stay calm, clean the nose gently, continue breastfeeding or offer enough fluids, encourage rest, and monitor breathing. Do not give antibiotics, cough medicine, or combination cold products to young children without professional advice.

Most common colds improve on their own. However, a persistent fever, poor feeding, worsening cough, or breathing difficulty requires medical assessment. Bluish skin, pauses in breathing, deep chest retractions, or reduced responsiveness are emergencies.
