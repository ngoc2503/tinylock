Your child suddenly feels hot, has flushed cheeks, or becomes unusually tired and irritable. You check their temperature and discover that they have a fever. Many parents immediately worry and try to bring the temperature down as quickly as possible, sometimes giving medicine or trying home remedies without knowing whether they are safe.

Fever is a natural response of the body to illness, most commonly infection. Many childhood fevers can be monitored and managed at home, but some require prompt medical assessment or emergency care.

So, what should you do when your child has a fever? When should you give fever medicine, and which warning signs should parents never ignore?

## 1. When Is a Child Considered to Have a Fever?

Fever occurs when the body's temperature rises above its normal range. A temperature of **38°C (100.4°F) or higher** is generally considered a fever in children, although readings may vary depending on the measurement method and body site.

Common signs include:
- The child's chest, back, or forehead feels hotter than usual.
- Flushed cheeks or sweating.
- Unusual tiredness, reduced activity, or irritability.
- Poor appetite or reduced interest in playing.
- Other symptoms such as coughing, a runny nose, sore throat, vomiting, or diarrhea.

Touching the forehead alone is not a reliable way to determine whether a child has a fever. Using an appropriate thermometer is more accurate.

### How to Measure Your Child's Temperature

Use a digital thermometer and follow the manufacturer's instructions.

- **Armpit measurement:** Place the thermometer tip in the center of the armpit, making sure it touches the skin. Keep the child's arm gently against the body until the reading is complete.
- **Ear or forehead measurement:** Use a device designed for that purpose and follow its instructions carefully.
- **Rectal measurement:** This may be used in some young infants when recommended by a healthcare professional.

Record the temperature, time, and measurement site so you can monitor changes. Do not automatically add or subtract a fixed number to convert between different measurement methods.

## 2. What Should You Do When Your Child Has a Fever?

If your child has a fever but remains alert, breathes normally, can drink fluids, and has no warning signs, you may be able to care for them at home.

### 2.1. Check Your Child's Overall Condition

Do not focus only on the thermometer reading. Observe whether:
- Your child is alert and responds normally.
- They are breathing comfortably.
- They can drink fluids or breastfeed.
- They are urinating normally.
- They have a rash, seizure, repeated vomiting, or unusual pain.

A child's overall condition matters. A child with a fever who remains alert, interacts normally, and drinks fluids may be less concerning than a child with a lower temperature who is unusually drowsy, has difficulty breathing, or responds abnormally.

### 2.2. Offer Plenty of Fluids

Fever can increase fluid loss, especially when a child is sweating, vomiting, or having diarrhea.

Parents should:
- Continue breastfeeding on demand.
- Continue offering formula or the child's usual milk if applicable.
- Offer older children water regularly, in small amounts.
- Consider oral rehydration solution for children with vomiting or diarrhea when recommended by a healthcare professional.

Do not force your child to drink a large amount at once. Offering smaller amounts more frequently may be easier.

### 2.3. Dress Your Child Comfortably

Dress your child in lightweight, comfortable clothing and keep the room at a pleasant temperature with good ventilation.

Avoid:
- Wrapping your child in multiple blankets or layers of clothing.
- Using ice or very cold water to cool them down.
- Rubbing the skin with alcohol.
- Trying to make your child sweat to reduce the fever.

If your child has chills, a light blanket may help them feel more comfortable. Avoid excessive wrapping.

### 2.4. Encourage Rest

Children often feel tired and less active when they have a fever. Give them opportunities to rest and sleep, and avoid activities that may exhaust them.

There is no need to force a child to stay in bed all day if they feel well enough for gentle activities. Let their condition guide their activity level.

### 2.5. Monitor Temperature and Other Symptoms

Check your child's temperature again when their condition changes or as advised by a healthcare professional. There is usually no need to measure it every few minutes if the child is stable.

Pay particular attention to:
- Difficulty waking or unusual drowsiness.
- Fast or difficult breathing.
- Refusal to drink or significantly reduced feeding.
- Reduced urination, dry mouth, or absence of tears when crying.
- An unusual rash or seizure.

## 3. When Should You Give Fever-Reducing Medicine?

Not every increase in temperature requires medicine.

The main purpose of fever-reducing medicine is to relieve discomfort and help a child rest. The goal is not necessarily to bring the temperature down to 37°C (98.6°F).

Two commonly used medicines are paracetamol (acetaminophen) and ibuprofen. However, the appropriate choice depends on the child's age, weight, medical history, and the specific product.

### 3.1. Paracetamol (Acetaminophen)

Paracetamol is commonly used to relieve pain and reduce fever in children.

When using it:
- Choose a product suitable for your child's age and weight.
- Check the medicine concentration on the packaging.
- Use the measuring device supplied with the medicine rather than an ordinary kitchen spoon.
- Follow the recommended interval between doses and the maximum daily amount.
- Check whether other medicines your child is taking also contain paracetamol to avoid accidental overdose.

Do not give paracetamol to a baby under 2 months old without medical direction. A baby under 3 months with a fever needs urgent medical assessment rather than home treatment alone.

### 3.2. Ibuprofen

Ibuprofen can also relieve pain and reduce fever, but it is not suitable for every child.

Do not give ibuprofen without appropriate medical advice if your child:
- Is younger than the age recommended on the product or has not been cleared to use it.
- Is dehydrated, vomiting frequently, or has diarrhea causing fluid loss.
- Has kidney disease or certain other medical conditions.
- Has chickenpox.
- Has a history of allergy or adverse reactions to this medicine.

**Important:** If dengue fever is suspected, do not give ibuprofen or aspirin unless specifically directed by a healthcare professional. These medicines may increase the risk of bleeding. Seek medical assessment for appropriate treatment.

### 3.3. Should You Alternate Paracetamol and Ibuprofen?

Do not give both medicines at the same time or alternate them without specific advice from a healthcare professional.

Alternating medicines can increase the risk of confusion about timing and dosage. It should only be considered when a healthcare professional provides clear instructions.

### 3.4. Never Give Aspirin to a Child for Fever

Do not use aspirin to treat a child's fever unless a doctor has prescribed it for a specific medical condition. Aspirin has been associated with Reye's syndrome, a rare but serious condition.

**Important:** The correct medicine dose depends on the child's weight, age, product concentration, and medical condition. Never copy another child's dosage or estimate a dose.

## 4. Common Mistakes to Avoid When Caring for a Child With a Fever

Some traditional remedies or common reactions may be ineffective or unsafe.

### 4.1. Trying to Bring the Temperature Down as Quickly as Possible

The temperature does not always need to return to normal. Fever medicine mainly helps a child feel more comfortable; it does not treat the underlying cause.

### 4.2. Using Ice or Alcohol Rubs

Do not use ice, alcohol, or sudden cooling methods. They may cause shivering, discomfort, or other harm.

### 4.3. Combining Multiple Medicines Without Checking Their Ingredients

Some cold and flu medicines already contain paracetamol. Giving an additional fever medicine with the same ingredient may cause an accidental overdose.

### 4.4. Giving Antibiotics Without a Prescription

Fever may be caused by viruses or bacteria. Antibiotics do not treat viral infections and should only be used when appropriately prescribed by a doctor.

### 4.5. Waiting Until the Fever Becomes Extremely High Before Seeking Help

Temperature alone does not determine how serious an illness is. A child may have a serious infection even without an extremely high fever, particularly newborns and young infants.

## 5. When Should You Seek Medical Care?

Parents should pay particular attention to the following warning signs.

### 5.1. Situations Requiring Prompt Medical Assessment

- **A baby younger than 3 months with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher:** The baby needs urgent medical assessment, even if no other symptoms are present.
- A baby aged 3–6 months with a temperature of 39°C (102.2°F) or higher: Seek prompt medical assessment.
- Fever lasting 5 days or longer.
- Signs of dehydration, such as reduced urination, dry mouth, sunken eyes, or no tears when crying.
- Fever accompanied by a rash, persistent vomiting, or worsening overall condition.

### 5.2. Warning Signs That Require Emergency Care

Take your child to the nearest emergency department or call emergency services if they have any of the following:
- Difficulty breathing, grunting, very rapid breathing, or chest retractions.
- Blue, gray, or unusually pale lips, tongue, or skin.
- Extreme drowsiness, difficulty waking, loss of responsiveness, or abnormal interaction.
- A seizure, especially the first seizure.
- A stiff neck or unusual sensitivity to light.
- A red or purple rash that does not fade when pressed.
- An unusual high-pitched cry that cannot be soothed or severe pain.
- Inability to drink or breastfeed, especially with signs of severe dehydration or exhaustion.

Do not wait for fever medicine to work before seeking emergency care when these signs are present.

## 6. What Should You Do If Your Child Has a Febrile Seizure?

Some children between approximately 6 months and 5 years of age may experience a febrile seizure. This can be frightening for parents, but knowing how to respond is important.

If your child has a seizure:
1. Place them on their side on a safe, flat surface, away from hard or sharp objects.
2. Remove nearby objects that could cause injury.
3. Note when the seizure starts and ends.
4. Do not hold down their arms or legs.
5. Do not put anything in their mouth, including a spoon, fingers, or medicine.
6. Do not try to give water or oral medicine during the seizure.

Call emergency services if this is the child's first seizure, the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, the child has difficulty breathing, or they do not regain consciousness afterward.

After the seizure, the child should be assessed by a healthcare professional to determine the cause.

Fever-reducing medicine has not been shown to prevent febrile seizures.

## 7. Final Thoughts

Fever is common in children and does not always indicate a dangerous illness. What matters most is knowing how to measure temperature, provide appropriate care, use medicine safely, and recognize warning signs.

Do not focus only on the number displayed on the thermometer. Pay attention to your child's breathing, alertness, ability to drink, and any unusual changes in their condition.

Most importantly, if a baby younger than 3 months has a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, or if a child develops any emergency warning signs, seek medical assessment immediately.
