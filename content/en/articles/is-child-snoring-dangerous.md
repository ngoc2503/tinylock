Snoring is fairly common in children, especially when they have a cold, a blocked nose or a respiratory infection. However, loud and frequent snoring, pauses in breathing or gasping during sleep should not be dismissed as a normal habit.

One condition parents should be aware of is **obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)**. It can interrupt a child's sleep and, if left untreated, may affect behaviour, attention and overall health.

## 1. Is snoring in children dangerous?

Not every case of childhood snoring is dangerous. Some children snore temporarily when they have a cold, nasal congestion or allergies that make it harder to breathe through the nose. The snoring often improves when the underlying problem gets better.

However, **loud and frequent snoring should not be ignored**. It may indicate that airflow through the upper airway is partially blocked during sleep.

Some children develop obstructive sleep apnea. In this condition, the airway becomes narrowed or repeatedly blocked during sleep. The child may struggle to breathe, experience brief pauses in breathing and wake up repeatedly without parents noticing.

If the condition continues, it may be associated with:

- Poor-quality sleep, frequent waking and restless nights.
- Difficulty concentrating and paying attention.
- Irritability, hyperactivity or changes in behaviour.
- Daytime tiredness or difficulty waking up in the morning.
- Problems with growth and other health concerns in more severe, prolonged cases.

Parents should therefore distinguish temporary snoring during a cold from persistent snoring or snoring accompanied by other unusual symptoms.

## 2. What causes children to snore?

Snoring usually occurs when airflow through the nose or throat is obstructed, causing soft tissues in the airway to vibrate as the child breathes.

Common causes include:

### 2.1. Nasal congestion from a cold or respiratory infection

When a child has a cold, rhinitis or a respiratory infection, the nasal lining may become swollen and produce more mucus. This makes it harder for air to pass through the nose. The child may breathe through their mouth and snore.

This type of snoring often lasts only while the child is unwell and may improve once nasal congestion clears.

### 2.2. Allergic rhinitis

Children who are allergic to household dust, dust mites, pollen, pet dander or other triggers may experience sneezing, an itchy nose, a runny nose and persistent nasal congestion.

When the nose is frequently blocked, the child may breathe through their mouth and snore during sleep.

### 2.3. Enlarged tonsils or adenoids

The tonsils are located on either side of the throat, while the adenoids are located behind the nose. These tissues are part of the immune system.

In some children, the tonsils or adenoids become enlarged and narrow the airway. This is an important cause of snoring and obstructive sleep apnea in children.

Parents may notice frequent snoring, mouth breathing, restless sleep or apparent difficulty breathing during sleep.

### 2.4. Overweight or obesity

Children who are overweight or obese may have more soft tissue around the neck and airway, which can increase the risk of airway narrowing during sleep.

However, children with a healthy weight can also snore or develop sleep apnea for other reasons.

### 2.5. Structural features of the airway

Some children have jaw, facial or airway structures that leave less room for airflow. Children with certain neuromuscular conditions or genetic syndromes may also be at increased risk.

If your child snores regularly, seek medical assessment rather than assuming it is simply due to their sleeping position or body type.

## 3. How can parents recognise abnormal snoring?

Parents should observe their child's breathing at night as well as their behaviour and energy levels during the day.

### Signs during sleep

- Loud snoring that occurs repeatedly throughout the week.
- Pauses in breathing followed by sudden gasps, snorts or choking sounds.
- Laboured breathing, or the chest and the area between the ribs pulling inward while breathing.
- Frequent mouth breathing.
- Restless sleep, frequent turning or waking.
- Excessive sweating during sleep.
- Unusual sleeping positions, such as extending the neck to make breathing easier.

### Signs during the day

- Difficulty waking up or frequent morning tiredness.
- Irritability or difficulty managing emotions.
- Difficulty concentrating at school or during play.
- Hyperactive behaviour or other changes in behaviour.
- Morning headaches.
- Unusual daytime sleepiness or falling asleep.
- Poor weight gain or growth concerns.

A single symptom does not establish a diagnosis of sleep apnea. However, if your child snores regularly and has one or more of these signs, arrange a medical assessment.

## 4. When could snoring indicate obstructive sleep apnea?

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) occurs when the upper airway becomes narrowed or repeatedly blocked while a child is asleep. The child may continue trying to breathe, but airflow into the lungs is reduced or temporarily stops.

Parents should pay particular attention if their child:

1. Snores loudly and regularly, not only during a cold.
2. Has pauses in breathing followed by gasping or sudden waking.
3. Sleeps restlessly, sweats excessively or frequently breathes through their mouth.
4. Is tired, irritable, has difficulty concentrating or shows behavioural changes during the day.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends evaluating children who snore regularly, especially when snoring is accompanied by signs or symptoms of OSA. A doctor may consider a sleep study to determine whether the condition is present and how severe it is.

**Important:** Snoring sounds or home observations alone cannot confirm sleep apnea. Diagnosis requires assessment by a healthcare professional.

## 5. When should a child who snores see a doctor or receive emergency care?

### Arrange a medical appointment

Take your child to a paediatrician or ear, nose and throat (ENT) specialist if:

- Your child snores regularly, around three or more nights per week.
- Snoring continues even when your child does not have a cold.
- Your child frequently breathes through their mouth or has persistent nasal congestion.
- You notice pauses in breathing, gasping or difficulty breathing during sleep.
- Sleep is repeatedly interrupted, or your child is tired or behaves differently during the day.
- Your child has growth or weight concerns alongside persistent snoring.

Do not wait for a specific number of weeks if you have already noticed pauses in breathing or difficulty breathing during sleep.

### Seek emergency medical care immediately

Call emergency services or take your child to the nearest emergency department if they have:

- Blue or purple lips, tongue or face.
- Prolonged pauses in breathing or repeated breathing pauses accompanied by colour changes.
- Severe difficulty breathing, chest retractions or extreme effort when breathing.
- Unusual drowsiness, difficulty waking or unresponsiveness.

Do not simply continue monitoring at home if your child shows signs of low oxygen or respiratory distress.

## 6. What should parents do when their child snores?

The right approach depends on the cause. Parents can take the following supportive steps:

### Step 1: Observe and record your child's sleep

Pay attention to:

- On which nights does your child snore, and how often?
- Is the snoring quiet or loud?
- Does your child pause breathing, gasp or breathe through their mouth?
- Does your child have a blocked or runny nose or frequent sneezing?
- Is your child tired, irritable or less attentive during the day?

If possible, record a short video showing the snoring and breathing pattern while your child sleeps. This may help the doctor, but it does not replace diagnostic testing.

### Step 2: Keep the sleeping environment clean

If your child has signs of allergies or nasal congestion:

- Clean the bedroom regularly.
- Wash bedding, pillowcases and soft toys regularly.
- Reduce dust, tobacco smoke and irritating fragrances.
- Keep the bedroom well ventilated and avoid excessive heat or dryness.

If you suspect an allergy to household dust, dust mites or pet dander, ask a healthcare professional how to reduce exposure appropriately.

### Step 3: Help relieve nasal congestion

If your child has a cold or a blocked nose, age-appropriate saline drops or spray may help clear nasal mucus.

For babies and young children who cannot blow their nose, ask a healthcare professional how to use gentle nasal suction safely.

Do not give nasal decongestant sprays, antihistamines, cough medicines or cold medicines without professional guidance.

### Step 4: Maintain a healthy weight and good sleep habits

If your child is overweight, talk to a doctor or dietitian about an age-appropriate nutrition and activity plan.

Also maintain a consistent bedtime, create a quiet sleeping environment and make sure your child gets enough sleep for their age.

These measures support overall health but do not replace medical assessment and treatment if the child has airway obstruction or sleep apnea.

## 7. How do doctors assess snoring in children?

During an appointment, the doctor will usually ask about how often the child snores, their breathing during sleep, daytime symptoms and medical history.

The assessment may include:

- Examining the nose, throat and tonsils.
- Assessing the adenoids, nasal congestion or allergies when appropriate.
- Checking height, weight and other risk factors.
- Asking about breathing pauses, gasping sounds or behavioural changes during sleep.

If obstructive sleep apnea is suspected, the doctor may recommend a polysomnography sleep study. This test monitors breathing, blood oxygen levels, heart rate and other sleep-related activity overnight.

Depending on the cause, treatment may include:

- Treating allergic rhinitis or other respiratory conditions.
- Monitoring and managing enlarged tonsils or adenoids.
- Supporting healthy weight management if the child is overweight.
- Tonsillectomy and/or adenoidectomy when recommended by a doctor.
- Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for selected children when medically indicated.

Not every child who snores needs surgery. Treatment should be based on the medical assessment and the underlying cause.

## 8. Common mistakes parents should avoid

When a child snores, parents should avoid:

- **Assuming snoring is always normal:** Frequent snoring can indicate a sleep-related breathing problem.
- **Buying medicines without advice:** Antihistamines, decongestants and cough or cold medicines may cause unwanted effects if used inappropriately.
- **Assuming the child has enlarged adenoids or tonsils:** A medical examination is needed.
- **Relying only on changing sleeping positions:** Changing position does not correct an underlying airway obstruction.
- **Ignoring pauses in breathing:** These require medical assessment, especially when accompanied by breathing difficulty or blue skin.

## 9. Conclusion

A child may snore temporarily because of a cold or nasal congestion. However, loud and frequent snoring, mouth breathing, restless sleep or pauses in breathing should prompt parents to seek medical assessment to rule out obstructive sleep apnea.

The most important thing is to observe both nighttime sleep and daytime behaviour. Identifying the cause early helps children receive appropriate care, improve their sleep quality and support healthy development.
