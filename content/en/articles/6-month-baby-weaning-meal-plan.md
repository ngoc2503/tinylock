When a baby is around 6 months old and shows signs of readiness, parents can begin introducing foods alongside breast milk or infant formula. At this stage, the goal is not to make the baby eat large amounts. It is to help them explore new tastes, textures and the skills needed to eat safely.

A 6-month-old baby's complementary diet should gradually include vegetables, fruits, iron-rich foods, protein and appropriate sources of fat. Start with small amounts, increase gradually according to the baby's abilities, and continue breastfeeding or offering infant formula as the main source of nutrition.

## 1. Is a 6-month-old baby ready for solid foods?

Around 6 months is an appropriate time for most babies to start complementary feeding. However, parents should look at the baby's developmental readiness rather than relying on age alone.

Signs that a baby may be ready include:

- They can keep their head and neck steady.
- They can sit upright with support.
- They can coordinate their eyes, hands and mouth to look at, pick up and bring food towards their mouth.
- They can swallow food rather than consistently pushing it out with their tongue.

If your baby was born prematurely, has developmental concerns or cannot yet control their head and neck well, ask a healthcare professional when to start.

**Important:** Do not introduce solid foods before 4 months. At around 6 months, breast milk or infant formula remains the baby's main source of nutrition during the early stages of complementary feeding.

## 2. What food groups should a 6-month-old's meal plan include?

At this stage, gradually introduce a variety of foods, with particular attention to iron- and zinc-rich options.

### Group 1: Vegetables

Vegetables provide vitamins, minerals and fibre. Suitable choices include:

- Pumpkin.
- Carrots.
- Sweet potatoes.
- Potatoes.
- Broccoli.
- Cauliflower.
- Spinach.
- Zucchini.

Wash vegetables thoroughly, cook until soft, and mash or purée them to a texture your baby can manage. Offer different vegetables rather than relying only on naturally sweet options.

### Group 2: Fruits

Fruit provides vitamins, fibre and a variety of flavours.

Options include:

- Ripe banana.
- Ripe avocado.
- Ripe papaya.
- Ripe mango.
- Soft, cooked pear.
- Soft, cooked apple.

Hard fruits such as apples and pears should be cooked until soft before mashing. Avoid hard or round pieces that could cause choking.

### Group 3: Iron-rich foods and protein

Iron is especially important when babies begin complementary feeding. Parents should introduce iron-rich foods early rather than relying on plain rice porridge or cereal alone.

Options include:

- Beef.
- Pork.
- Chicken.
- Fish with all bones carefully removed.
- Fully cooked eggs, including both yolk and white.
- Lentils.
- Peas.
- Beans that have been thoroughly cooked and mashed.

Meat, fish and legumes should be thoroughly cooked and finely minced, mashed or puréed to suit the baby's eating skills. Pairing iron-rich foods with vegetables or fruits containing vitamin C may help the body absorb iron.

### Group 4: Starchy foods

Starchy foods provide energy for growth and activity.

Choices include:

- Infant rice cereal.
- Soft-cooked rice porridge.
- Well-cooked oatmeal.
- Sweet potatoes.
- Potatoes.
- Iron-fortified infant cereals.

Do not rely on plain rice porridge or rice cereal for too long. Gradually combine these foods with iron-rich ingredients, protein and vegetables.

### Group 5: Healthy fats

Fat provides energy and helps the body absorb fat-soluble vitamins.

Small amounts of the following may be included:

- Olive oil.
- Soybean oil.
- Sesame oil, if sesame has been safely introduced.
- Mashed avocado.
- Smooth nut or seed butter with no added salt or sugar, once introduced safely.

There is no need to add large amounts of oil. Use a moderate amount appropriate for the baby's portion.

## 3. A 7-day sample meal plan for a 6-month-old baby

At the beginning, a baby may need only one small complementary meal a day. As the baby becomes more comfortable with food, parents can gradually increase the number of meals, working towards 2–3 meals a day during the 6–8-month period according to the baby's needs and abilities.

The following is a sample plan, not a strict schedule. Your baby does not need to finish every portion or follow the exact order.

| Day | Suggested meal | Main ingredients |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Thin rice cereal | Cooked rice cereal |
| Day 2 | Mashed pumpkin | Steamed pumpkin |
| Day 3 | Oatmeal and banana | Well-cooked oats, ripe banana |
| Day 4 | Chicken and pumpkin porridge | Rice, chicken, pumpkin |
| Day 5 | Mashed sweet potato and lentils | Sweet potato, cooked lentils |
| Day 6 | Beef and broccoli porridge | Rice, beef, broccoli |
| Day 7 | Mashed avocado and plain yoghurt | Ripe avocado, plain unsweetened yoghurt |

**How to use this meal plan:**

- If your baby has only just started solids, introduce each food in a small amount and increase gradually as they become comfortable.
- You do not need to introduce several new ingredients on the same day.
- Introduce common allergens such as egg, fish, soy, peanut and dairy separately and safely.
- Choose pasteurised plain yoghurt with no added sugar. Do not use cow's milk as the baby's main drink before 12 months.
- If your baby does not accept a food, try again another time rather than forcing them to eat.

## 4. How much and how often should a 6-month-old eat?

A baby's digestive system and eating skills are still developing. Start with a very small amount, such as a few teaspoons, and gradually increase according to the baby's appetite and ability.

Some general principles:

- **At the beginning:** You can start with one small meal a day.
- **As your baby gets used to food:** Gradually increase the amount and number of meals.
- **From 6 to 8 months:** WHO recommends about 2–3 complementary meals a day, depending on the child's needs and abilities.
- **Continue milk feeds:** Breastfeed on demand or continue offering suitable infant formula.

There is no single portion size that is right for every 6-month-old. Watch for signs of hunger and fullness:

- Opening the mouth, leaning towards food or showing interest may indicate hunger.
- Turning the head away, closing the mouth, pushing the spoon away or losing interest may indicate fullness.
- Do not force your baby to finish a bowl or distract them with a phone to make them eat more.

## 5. How to prepare food for a 6-month-old

Food should be thoroughly cooked, soft and prepared in a texture appropriate for the baby's eating skills.

### Porridge or infant cereal

- Wash the rice and cook it until very soft.
- Mash or blend it to a suitable consistency.
- As your baby becomes more skilled, gradually increase thickness and texture rather than keeping all food completely smooth for too long.

### Mashed vegetables

- Wash and peel vegetables when necessary.
- Steam or boil until soft.
- Mash or purée to a suitable texture.
- A little cooking water or correctly prepared breast milk or formula can be used to adjust consistency.

### Meat, fish and eggs

- Cook meat and fish thoroughly.
- Remove all fish bones and hard cartilage.
- Cook eggs thoroughly.
- Mince or mash the food finely, then combine it with porridge or vegetables.

As your baby develops eating skills, gradually move from smooth purées to mashed, lumpy and soft finger foods they can manage. Do not keep food completely smooth if your baby is ready to learn to chew and handle different textures.

## 6. Can a 6-month-old eat eggs, fish and common allergenic foods?

Yes. Foods that can trigger allergic reactions, such as eggs, peanuts, fish, cow's milk, wheat, soy and shellfish, can be introduced from around 6 months when the baby is ready for complementary feeding.

Parents should:

- Introduce each new allergenic food separately and in a small amount so that reactions are easier to identify.
- Offer new foods during the day when you can observe your baby.
- If the food is tolerated, continue including it regularly in a safe form.
- Never offer whole peanuts, whole nuts or a thick spoonful of nut butter because of the choking risk.
- If your baby has severe eczema, a known food allergy or a family history of food allergies, speak with a healthcare professional before introducing common allergens.

If your baby develops hives, swelling of the lips or face, vomiting or unusual coughing after eating, stop offering the suspected food and contact a healthcare professional for advice. If your baby has difficulty breathing, severe wheezing, swelling of the tongue or throat, turns blue, becomes floppy or shows signs of a severe reaction, seek emergency medical help immediately.

## 7. Foods a 6-month-old should not eat

To help keep your baby safe, avoid or limit the following:

- **Honey:** Never give honey to a baby under 12 months because of the risk of infant botulism.
- **Salt and sugar:** Do not add salt, sugar or salty and sweet seasonings to your baby's food.
- **Cow's milk as the main drink:** Do not use plain cow's milk instead of breast milk or infant formula before 12 months. Small amounts may be used in cooking from around 6 months.
- **Whole nuts:** Whole peanuts, cashews, almonds and other nuts can cause choking.
- **Hard, round or slippery foods:** Examples include raw carrots, hard raw apples, whole grapes and sliced round pieces of sausage.
- **Raw or undercooked foods:** Undercooked eggs, meat, fish and seafood may increase the risk of infection.
- **Soft drinks, juice and sugary drinks:** These are unnecessary for a 6-month-old and may reduce the baby's interest in milk feeds.

## 8. Important tips for safe complementary feeding

**Use a safe sitting position:** Your baby should sit upright in a suitable high chair with a safety harness. Do not feed your baby while lying down, crawling, walking or sitting in a moving vehicle.

**Supervise every meal:** Never leave your baby alone while eating. Learn the difference between gagging as a baby explores food and choking. If your baby cannot breathe, cry or cough effectively, provide appropriate choking first aid and call emergency services.

**Maintain food hygiene:** Wash your hands before preparing food and feeding your baby. Use clean utensils, store food safely and do not reuse food that has been in contact with your baby's saliva.

**Do not force your baby to eat:** Every baby adjusts at a different pace. Some foods may need to be offered several times before a baby accepts them. Be patient, encourage exploration and respect signs of fullness.

**Do not replace milk feeds with solid food:** During the first year, breast milk or infant formula remains the main source of nutrition. Complementary feeding gradually adds foods and eating skills.

## 9. When should you ask a healthcare professional?

Talk to your baby's healthcare professional if your baby:

- Was born prematurely or has a medical condition that affects feeding.
- Cannot keep their head steady or sit with appropriate support.
- Frequently coughs, chokes, has difficulty swallowing or vomits during meals.
- Shows signs of an allergic reaction after eating a particular food.
- Feeds poorly, is not gaining weight well or shows signs of dehydration.
- Refuses most foods over an extended period, or you are concerned about their feeding skills or development.

If your baby has difficulty breathing, turns blue, becomes unusually drowsy or has signs of a severe allergic reaction, seek emergency medical care immediately.

## 10. Conclusion

A 6-month-old's meal plan does not need to be complicated. Parents can start with simple foods such as oatmeal, mashed pumpkin, sweet potato, avocado, chicken, beef, fish and properly prepared legumes.

The most important things are to offer a varied diet, prioritise iron-rich foods, gradually introduce new textures, introduce common allergens safely and always supervise meals.

Let your baby explore food at their own pace. Do not force them to eat, and continue breastfeeding or offering infant formula throughout the first year.
