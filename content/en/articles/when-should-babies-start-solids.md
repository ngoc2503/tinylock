Starting solid foods, also called **complementary feeding**, is an important stage in a baby's development. It introduces foods in addition to breast milk or infant formula and helps babies explore new tastes, textures, and feeding skills.

However, **you should not decide when to start solids simply because your baby watches others eat or frequently puts their hands in their mouth**. Both age and developmental readiness matter.

## 1. When should a baby start solid foods?

Most babies should start solid foods **at around 6 months of age**, when they have developed the skills needed to manage food. Introducing solid foods before 4 months is not recommended.

During the first 6 months of life, breast milk or infant formula provides most of a baby's nutritional needs. At around 6 months, babies need additional energy and nutrients, particularly iron, so appropriate complementary foods should be introduced while breast milk or infant formula continues.

**Remember:** Babies do not need teeth to start solids. Head control, supported sitting, and the ability to coordinate their mouth movements to swallow food are more important than whether their teeth have appeared.

## 2. Five signs your baby may be ready for solid foods

At around 6 months, look for the following signs.

### 2.1. Your baby can keep their head and neck steady

Your baby can hold their head upright and stable while sitting in a feeding position. This is an important skill for safer feeding.

### 2.2. Your baby can sit upright with appropriate support

Your baby can maintain a reasonably stable sitting position without repeatedly falling forward or sideways.

### 2.3. Your baby coordinates their eyes, hands, and mouth

Your baby looks at food, reaches for it, and brings objects toward their mouth. These behaviors show developing coordination.

### 2.4. Your baby opens their mouth when food is offered

Your baby may open their mouth when an adult brings a spoon toward them, showing willingness to accept food.

### 2.5. Your baby swallows food instead of consistently pushing it out with their tongue

Your baby is developing the oral skills needed to manage food. The texture must still be appropriate for their abilities.

Do not rely on a single sign alone. **Chewing on hands, waking more often at night, wanting more milk, or watching other people eat does not by itself mean a baby is ready for solid foods.**

## 3. How to start feeding your baby solid foods

### 3.1. Start with small amounts

Offer a small amount of soft, mashed, or puréed food with a texture suited to your baby's abilities.

During the first few days, the main goal is to help your baby explore new tastes, textures, and feeding skills. You do not need to make your baby finish a full serving.

### 3.2. Prioritize nutrient-rich foods

Introduce foods rich in iron and zinc early, such as:

- Appropriately prepared, thoroughly cooked meat.
- Fish suitable for the baby's age, with all bones carefully removed.
- Thoroughly cooked eggs.
- Beans and other legumes prepared until soft.
- Iron-fortified infant cereals.

Also offer a variety of vegetables, fruits, and other appropriate foods to provide a diverse diet.

For most babies, there is no single required order for introducing foods. Choose foods that are age-appropriate, safely prepared, and nutritionally valuable.

### 3.3. Gradually increase texture and variety

At first, your baby may do best with smooth, soft, mashed, or puréed foods. As feeding skills develop, gradually introduce thicker mashed foods, soft lumps, and appropriately prepared soft finger foods.

Avoid keeping your baby on smooth purées for longer than necessary once they are ready for other textures. Gradually exploring suitable textures helps babies develop their chewing and food-handling skills.

### 3.4. Continue breast milk or infant formula

From 6 to 12 months, breast milk or infant formula remains an important source of nutrition.

Solid foods complement milk feeds. They should not suddenly replace most milk feeds when your baby is just beginning to eat.

### 3.5. Follow your baby's hunger and fullness cues

Keep your baby upright and supervise them closely throughout meals.

Encourage your baby to explore food without pressure. If your baby turns away, closes their mouth, pushes the spoon away, or loses interest, pause and try again another time.

## 4. Important food-safety rules

### 4.1. Always supervise your baby while eating

Never leave your baby eating alone. Babies should sit upright during meals and should not eat while lying down, crawling, walking, or riding in a moving vehicle.

### 4.2. Avoid foods that can cause choking

Avoid whole nuts, popcorn, hard sweets, whole grapes, and other hard, round, slippery foods that may block the airway.

Cut, mash, or otherwise prepare foods to match your baby's feeding abilities. Pay particular attention to foods with shapes that can easily become lodged in the airway.

### 4.3. Do not add salt or sugar

Limit foods high in salt or added sugar, and avoid sugary drinks.

**Never give honey to a child under 12 months old**, including honey mixed into food or drinks, because of the risk of infant botulism.

### 4.4. Introduce allergenic foods appropriately

Foods such as thoroughly cooked eggs and peanut in a safe, age-appropriate form can be introduced when complementary feeding begins.

Offer a small amount of one new allergenic food at a time so that you can recognize a possible reaction. Never give whole peanuts to a baby.

If your baby has severe eczema, an existing food allergy, or other high-risk factors, ask your healthcare professional how to introduce allergenic foods safely.

If your baby develops difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, collapse, or other signs of a severe allergic reaction after eating, call emergency services immediately.

### 4.5. Maintain good food hygiene

Wash your hands before preparing food and feeding your baby. Use clean utensils, cook foods appropriately, and store food safely.

## 5. What if your baby does not seem ready?

If your baby is approaching 6 months but cannot keep their head steady, cannot sit with appropriate support, or appears to have difficulty swallowing, do not force them to eat.

Speak with your baby's healthcare professional to assess their development and determine the appropriate next steps.

Babies born prematurely and babies with growth concerns, swallowing difficulties, or other medical conditions may need individualized advice about when and how to start complementary feeding.

## 6. When should you seek medical advice?

Talk with a healthcare professional if your baby:

- Frequently coughs or chokes during meals.
- Appears to have difficulty swallowing or managing food.
- Experiences repeated vomiting during feeding.
- Shows signs of a reaction to food.
- Is not growing as expected, or you have concerns about their feeding abilities.

If your baby develops difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, collapse, or other signs of a severe allergic reaction after eating, call emergency services immediately.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

### Can I start solid foods at 4 months?

Do not start solid foods at 4 months without medical guidance. General recommendations advise starting at around 6 months, and introducing solids before 4 months is not recommended. If there is a specific medical reason to consider a different approach, speak with your baby's healthcare professional.

### Can a baby start solids before getting teeth?

Yes. Babies do not need teeth to start complementary feeding. What matters is adequate head control, sitting with appropriate support, and the ability to swallow food with a suitable texture.

### Does my baby need to eat a lot at first?

No. Start with small amounts and gradually increase them according to your baby's abilities and hunger and fullness cues. Do not force-feed, and continue breast milk or infant formula.

### Must babies start with cereal before trying other foods?

Not necessarily. Babies can be introduced to a variety of age-appropriate foods. Prioritize iron-rich foods, safe textures, and nutritional variety.
