When sleeping, babies may frequently squirm, pull their legs up, grimace, turn red in the face, or make little grunting noises. These behaviors can worry parents. Some may think their baby has gas, indigestion, a calcium deficiency, or poor sleep, and try massage, supplements, or changes to the baby's diet.

However, these behaviors are not always signs of a medical problem. During the first months of life, a baby's nervous system and motor coordination are still developing. Babies may move, make noises, or change facial expressions while remaining asleep.

So, is it normal for a baby to squirm, turn red, and grunt during sleep? When should parents seek medical attention?

## 1. Is it normal for babies to squirm, turn red, and grunt during sleep?

In many cases, these are normal behaviors, especially in newborns and babies during their first few months.

Young children's sleep is not always quiet or motionless. During light sleep or transitions between sleep stages, babies may:

- Squirm, stretch their arms and legs, or pull their knees up.
- Grimace, frown, smile, or move their lips.
- Make little grunting, snuffling, or squeaking sounds.
- Startle or briefly jerk their arms and legs.
- Move around, make a short crying sound, and then continue sleeping.
- Briefly turn red when straining or making an effort.

If your baby is breathing normally, has pink lips and tongue, feeds well, is alert when awake, and is growing appropriately, these brief behaviors are usually not a cause for concern.

However, grunting with every breath, labored breathing, or unusual changes in skin color should be assessed immediately because these may indicate breathing difficulties.

## 2. Why do babies squirm and grunt during sleep?

There are several reasons why babies may behave this way. Some are related to normal sleep activity and development, while others may involve digestion or health conditions.

### Cause 1: Active sleep

Newborns spend a significant portion of their sleep in active sleep, which is similar to REM sleep.

During this stage, babies may:
- Move their arms and legs.
- Make facial expressions, smile, or move their lips.
- Make small noises.
- Have slightly irregular breathing for brief periods.
- Squirm without fully waking up.

This is part of normal sleep development. Parents do not need to wake their baby just because they notice some movement or a few small grunts, as long as the baby otherwise appears well.

### Cause 2: An immature nervous system and developing motor coordination

A newborn's nervous system is still developing. Babies cannot fully control all their body movements, so they may stretch, pull their legs up, squirm, or briefly jerk while sleeping.

These movements and reflexes generally become less noticeable as babies grow and gain better control of their bodies.

### Cause 3: Straining to pass stool or gas

Some babies turn red, pull their legs up, tighten their abdominal muscles, grunt, or cry when trying to have a bowel movement.

In babies younger than 9 months, if they strain or cry for at least about 10 minutes before passing a stool or trying unsuccessfully, but their stools remain soft, this may be a condition called **infant dyschezia**.

It occurs because babies have not yet learned to coordinate increased abdominal pressure with relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles to pass stool. It is usually a developmental coordination issue, not the same as constipation.

The key differences are:
- **Infant dyschezia:** The baby strains, turns red, or cries, but the stool is usually soft.
- **Constipation:** The stool is usually dry, hard, or pellet-like, and passing it may be painful.

Infant dyschezia usually improves as babies grow and learn to coordinate the muscles involved in bowel movements. Parents should not use rectal stimulation, insert a thermometer into the anus, or give laxatives without medical advice.

### Cause 4: Gas or abdominal discomfort

Young babies may swallow air while feeding, especially if they do not latch properly or use an unsuitable bottle-feeding position. Gas in the digestive tract may make them uncomfortable, causing them to pull their legs up, squirm, or grunt.

Other possible signs include:
- A belly that seems more swollen than usual.
- Pulling the legs toward the abdomen.
- Frequent passing of gas.
- Fussiness after feeding.

However, not every episode of squirming is caused by gas. If your baby feeds well, has a soft belly, passes stools normally, and shows no other unusual signs, intervention is often unnecessary.

### Cause 5: Discomfort during sleep

Several factors may make babies squirm or grunt more often:

- A wet or dirty diaper.
- A room that is too hot or too cold.
- Tight or uncomfortable clothing.
- Hunger or the need to feed.
- Too much noise or light in the sleeping environment.
- Tiredness or overstimulation before bedtime.

Check these factors if your baby repeatedly wakes up, cries, or has difficulty settling back to sleep.

## 3. What should parents do when their baby squirmes, turns red, and grunts during sleep?

If your baby is otherwise healthy, feeds well, and has no signs of breathing difficulty, you can try the following:

### Step 1: Observe before intervening

Do not immediately pick up, wake, or reposition your baby just because they squirm, grimace, or make a few small noises.

Observe:
- Is your baby actually awake, or still sleeping?
- Is the breathing pattern normal?
- Are the lips and tongue pink?
- Does your baby seem to be in prolonged pain, or are the movements brief?

If your baby settles back into sleep and has no unusual symptoms, you can let them continue sleeping.

### Step 2: Provide a safe sleeping environment

- Place your baby on their back at the beginning of every sleep.
- Use a firm, flat mattress that fits the crib or bassinet properly.
- Keep pillows, thick blankets, stuffed toys, and other soft objects out of the sleeping area.
- Do not elevate your baby's head or use an inclined or anti-reflux sleep pillow.
- Prevent overheating and dress your baby appropriately for the room temperature.
- Use a separate infant sleep surface, ideally in the same room as the parents.

If your baby can roll over, continue placing them on their back at the start of sleep. Stop swaddling once your baby shows signs of trying to roll.

### Step 3: Support your baby after feeding

If your baby often squirms or seems uncomfortable after feeding, you can:

- Check the feeding position and latch.
- Allow breaks during feeding if needed.
- Gently burp your baby if they seem to have swallowed a lot of air.
- Hold your baby upright while they are awake for a short period after feeding if they frequently spit up.

There is no need to force burping if your baby is comfortable. When placing your baby to sleep, always use a safe, flat surface and place them on their back. Do not use side or stomach sleeping to prevent spit-up.

### Step 4: Maintain an appropriate sleep routine

- Keep sleep and wake times reasonably consistent.
- Reduce light and noise before bedtime.
- Create a gentle bedtime routine, such as changing the diaper, feeding, cuddling, or singing.
- Avoid letting your baby become overtired or overstimulated before sleep.

### Step 5: Monitor unusual symptoms

Parents can record or take a short video of concerning episodes to show a healthcare professional.

Pay attention to:
- When the squirming and grunting occur.
- Whether they happen during sleep or while trying to pass stool.
- How long they last and how often they occur.
- Whether your baby feeds poorly, vomits, has a fever, or develops changes in bowel movements.
- Breathing patterns and the color of the lips and tongue during an episode.

A video may help a doctor understand what is happening, but never delay emergency care to record a video if your baby has danger signs.

## 4. When are squirming and facial redness signs of a problem?

Parents should arrange a medical assessment if their baby has any of the following:

- Frequent discomfort, prolonged crying, or difficulty being soothed.
- Poor feeding, refusing feeds, or a significant reduction in intake.
- Frequent vomiting, green vomit, or blood in vomit.
- A swollen or painful abdomen, or inability to pass stool or gas with other unusual symptoms.
- Blood in the stool, or persistent hard and painful bowel movements.
- Poor weight gain or signs of dehydration, such as fewer wet diapers or a dry mouth.
- Repeated unusual movements, stiffening, continuous jerking, or changes in responsiveness.
- Fever or other unusual changes in health.

A baby younger than 3 months with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher needs prompt medical assessment.

## 5. Signs of breathing difficulty that require emergency care

Do not confuse ordinary sleep grunts with grunting caused by breathing difficulty.

**Call your local emergency number or seek emergency medical care immediately if your baby has any of the following:**

- Grunts with nearly every breath, especially when breathing out.
- Unusually fast breathing or obvious effort to breathe.
- The chest or area below the ribs pulls inward when breathing in.
- Flaring nostrils or head bobbing with each breath.
- Blue, purple, or gray lips, tongue, or face.
- Concerning pauses in breathing, especially when accompanied by a color change or limpness.
- Unusual drowsiness, difficulty waking, or reduced responsiveness.

Repeated grunting with each breath may indicate that a baby is working hard to maintain airflow and exchange oxygen. This is not normal sleep behavior.

## 6. Common mistakes parents should avoid

**Giving calcium or vitamin D supplements because they think squirming means a deficiency:** Squirming is not a specific sign of calcium or vitamin D deficiency. Supplementation should be based on the child's needs, diet, and medical guidance.

**Using medicines or digestive supplements without advice:** Do not give anti-gas medicine, digestive enzymes, or laxatives simply because your baby grunts or squirms.

**Stimulating the anus to help the baby pass stool:** Do not routinely use a thermometer, cotton swab, or other object to stimulate the anus. This does not help babies learn natural bowel coordination.

**Waking the baby repeatedly:** If your baby only moves or makes small noises but continues breathing normally and remains asleep, there is no need to wake them repeatedly.

**Using stomach sleeping or pillows to help the baby sleep better:** This is not a safe solution. Babies should be placed on their backs on a firm, flat sleep surface with no soft objects around them.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

**Does a newborn who squirms and turns red during sleep have a calcium deficiency?**

You cannot diagnose calcium deficiency based only on squirming or facial redness during sleep. These may be normal sleep movements or signs of straining to pass stool. If your baby has seizures, unusual trembling, poor feeding, or other concerning symptoms, seek medical assessment.

**Should I wake my baby if they grunt or make little noises during sleep?**

If your baby remains asleep, breathes normally, has pink lips, and shows no signs of serious discomfort, you do not need to wake them. Young babies can make many sounds during light sleep.

**If my baby turns red, strains, and passes soft stools, is that constipation?**

Not necessarily. Babies younger than 9 months may experience infant dyschezia. They may strain, turn red, or cry even though their stools remain soft. Constipation is more commonly associated with hard, dry stools and difficulty passing them.

**Can frequent squirming affect my baby's sleep?**

If your baby only moves during light sleep and then continues sleeping, with no prolonged discomfort and normal growth, this is usually not concerning. If your baby frequently wakes, cries, or feeds poorly, discuss possible causes with a healthcare professional.

**Are grunting sounds during sleep dangerous?**

Occasional, quiet grunts that are not continuous may be normal. However, grunting with every breath, fast breathing, chest retractions, or bluish discoloration are danger signs that require emergency care.

## Conclusion

Babies who squirm, turn red, grimace, and grunt during sleep in their first months of life are often showing normal physiological behaviors related to light sleep, nervous system development, or straining to pass stool.

Parents should observe breathing, lip and tongue color, feeding, and growth rather than assuming that squirming alone means the baby is ill. Do not give calcium supplements, medicines, or rectal interventions without medical guidance.

Most importantly, if your baby grunts with every breath, struggles to breathe, turns blue, feeds poorly, or is difficult to wake, seek emergency medical care immediately.
