Every child develops at their own pace. Some children begin saying their first words relatively early, while others need more time to express their needs through speech. However, if a child communicates less than expected for their age, has difficulty understanding language, or rarely uses gestures to communicate, parents should pay attention to their language development.

A speech delay does not necessarily mean that a child has a serious medical condition. However, it may indicate that the child needs an assessment of their hearing, language skills, or overall development.

What should parents do if their child is a late talker? How can they support language development at home, and when should they seek professional help?

## 1. What Is a Speech or Language Delay?

A speech delay is a commonly used term when a child's spoken communication develops more slowly than expected for their age.

To understand a child's needs, it is important to distinguish between two areas:

- **Expressive language:** How a child uses words, combines them into sentences, and communicates their needs.
- **Receptive language:** How well a child understands spoken language, follows simple instructions, and recognizes familiar words.

Some children understand language relatively well but say very few words. Others have difficulty both expressing themselves and understanding what people say. These situations are not identical and may require different assessments.

Parents should also observe how their child communicates through eye contact, gestures, pointing, facial expressions, and actions.

## 2. Signs That a Child May Have a Language Delay

The milestones below can help parents recognize when they may need to pay closer attention to their child's communication. They are not intended to diagnose a developmental condition.

### 2.1. Children Aged 0–12 Months

During the first year, babies gradually develop communication skills before they begin using meaningful words.

Parents should pay attention if a baby:
- Rarely responds to sounds or voices.
- Makes very few babbling sounds as they grow.
- Rarely looks toward people who are interacting with them.
- Makes little use of sounds, facial expressions, or gestures to get attention.

These signs do not establish that a baby has a speech delay, but they may indicate a need to assess hearing and communication development.

### 2.2. Children Aged 12–18 Months

During this stage, many children begin using simple words to name familiar people or express their needs.

Parents should seek advice if a child:
- Is not using meaningful words by around 16–18 months.
- Rarely imitates sounds or gestures.
- Does not point to show what they want or share interest.
- Has difficulty understanding familiar, simple instructions.

Consider the child's communication, language comprehension, and progress over time rather than counting words alone.

### 2.3. Children Aged 18–24 Months

During this period, children's vocabularies often grow, and they begin combining words to express ideas.

Signs that deserve attention include:
- A very limited vocabulary or little progress in learning new words.
- Not yet combining two meaningful words by around 24 months.
- Difficulty understanding simple, familiar instructions.
- Limited use of words or gestures to request, share, and interact.

The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) identifies fewer than 50 expressive words and no two-word combinations at around 24 months as commonly used indicators of late language emergence. This is a screening reference, not a diagnosis by itself.

### 2.4. Children Aged 2–3 Years

Children gradually begin using short phrases and sentences to make requests, describe events, and answer simple questions.

Parents should seek further guidance if a child:
- Mainly communicates by pulling adults' hands or crying instead of using appropriate words or gestures.
- Does not use short phrases to express needs.
- Has difficulty understanding familiar, simple directions.
- Has significant difficulty communicating with familiar people despite opportunities to interact.

### 2.5. Children Older Than 3 Years

If a child older than 3 continues to have substantial difficulty expressing ideas, understanding language, or communicating with others, parents should arrange an assessment.

During the preschool years, communication involves more than the number of words a child knows. It also includes combining words into sentences, answering questions, describing events, and participating in conversations.

**Important:** These age ranges are general guides. If you are concerned about your child's development, you do not need to wait until a particular age to seek professional advice.

## 3. Why Might a Child Have a Speech or Language Delay?

There are several possible reasons for delayed speech. Parents should not automatically assume that a child is late to talk because adults do not talk to them enough or because they watch screens.

Possible factors include:

### 3.1. Hearing Difficulties

Children need to hear sounds and speech to learn language. Hearing difficulties may affect their ability to recognize sounds, imitate words, and develop vocabulary.

Even if a child responds to some sounds, this does not completely rule out a hearing problem.

A hearing assessment is therefore an important part of evaluating a child with possible language delay.

### 3.2. Language Development Difficulties

Some children have difficulty learning new words, understanding sentences, or using language to express their thoughts.

A child may have difficulties mainly with expressive language or with both understanding and using language.

### 3.3. Differences in Overall Development

Speech and language delays may occur alongside differences in social interaction, behavior, play, or other developmental skills.

For example, some autistic children have differences in social communication and language development. However, a speech delay alone does not mean that a child is autistic.

Identifying the cause requires a comprehensive assessment rather than relying on a single behavior.

### 3.4. Opportunities for Communication

Children learn language by listening, observing, interacting, and practicing with people around them.

If a child has few opportunities for back-and-forth conversation, interactive play, or responsive communication with adults, language learning may be more challenging.

However, limited interaction should not automatically be assumed to be the sole cause of a speech delay.

### 3.5. Growing Up With More Than One Language

Children growing up in families that use two or more languages may distribute their vocabulary across those languages. This does not automatically indicate a language delay.

When assessing a multilingual child, professionals should consider the child's communication abilities across all the languages they use, as well as their exposure to and opportunities to practice each language.

Parents do not necessarily need to stop speaking their home language to help their child learn to communicate.

## 4. What Should Parents Do If Their Child Is a Late Talker?

If you notice signs of a possible language delay, take two complementary steps: seek professional assessment when appropriate and create a supportive communication environment at home.

### 4.1. Arrange an Early Assessment

Do not simply wait for your child to start talking when they are older if they show signs of delayed language development.

You can begin by discussing your concerns with a pediatrician. Depending on the child's needs, the doctor may recommend:
- A hearing assessment.
- An evaluation of receptive and expressive language.
- An assessment of other developmental areas.
- Consultation with a speech-language therapist or child development specialist.

Early assessment helps identify which areas need support and which interventions may be appropriate.

### 4.2. Talk During Everyday Activities

You do not need to turn every moment into a lesson. Use ordinary routines to help your child hear and practice language.

For example:
- During bath time: "Mommy is washing your hands."
- At mealtime: "Would you like a banana or an apple?"
- While getting dressed: "This is your blue shirt."
- During a walk: "Look! There is a bus!"

Speak clearly at a natural pace and give your child time to respond.

### 4.3. Follow Your Child's Interests

If your child is looking at a toy car, start by talking about the car rather than repeatedly asking them to name unrelated objects.

For example, if your child pushes the car across the floor, you can say, "The car is going!" and continue playing in a way that interests them.

Following a child's attention and responding meaningfully creates opportunities to learn new words in context.

### 4.4. Expand on Your Child's Words

When your child says one word, repeat it and expand it into a short phrase or sentence.

For example:

- Child: "Ball."
- Parent: "Yes, a big ball."
- Child: "Mommy carry."
- Parent: "You want Mommy to carry you."

You do not need to make your child repeat every word perfectly. Provide natural language models that they can learn from over time.

### 4.5. Read Picture Books Together Every Day

Choose books with clear pictures, simple illustrations, and content appropriate for your child's age.

You can:
- Point to pictures and name familiar objects.
- Imitate animal sounds or vehicle noises.
- Ask simple questions when your child is ready.
- Pause so your child can point, make sounds, or respond in their own way.

You do not need to read every word on every page. The important part is the interaction between you and your child.

### 4.6. Encourage All Forms of Communication

Communication is not limited to spoken words. Children may use eye contact, pointing, gestures, facial expressions, sounds, or pictures to express their needs.

Respond to these attempts and create opportunities for your child to initiate communication.

If your child needs gestures, pictures, or another form of augmentative and alternative communication, discuss suitable options with a professional. These methods should not be treated as obstacles to spoken language development.

### 4.7. Limit Passive Screen Use

Children benefit from opportunities to interact with real people through back-and-forth communication.

Prioritize conversation, interactive play, reading, and everyday activities. Do not rely on phones or videos as the primary way to teach a child to talk.

If your child watches content on a screen, watch together when possible and talk about what is happening rather than leaving the child to watch alone for long periods.

## 5. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Some approaches may reduce opportunities for communication or delay access to appropriate support.

- **Waiting for the child to talk without an assessment:** Some children catch up with their peers, but this cannot be predicted for every child.
- **Constantly forcing the child to repeat words:** Turning communication into a test may cause frustration or reduce interest.
- **Focusing only on the number of words:** Understanding, gestures, social interaction, and progress over time also matter.
- **Comparing the child with siblings or peers:** Every child has their own developmental profile.
- **Giving medicines or supplements to make the child talk faster:** Do not use products without an appropriate professional assessment and recommendation.
- **Assuming the child is autistic or has another condition:** A professional assessment is needed to identify the cause and support needs.
- **Assuming that growing up bilingual causes a speech disorder:** Using multiple languages does not automatically cause a language disorder.

## 6. When Should Parents Seek Professional Help?

Parents should discuss their concerns with a pediatrician or qualified specialist if:
- The child is not meeting expected communication milestones.
- The child does not consistently respond to sounds or voices.
- The child rarely uses gestures to communicate or has difficulty understanding language.
- At around 24 months, the child has a very limited expressive vocabulary or does not combine two meaningful words.
- The child has significant difficulty communicating with others.
- Language development appears to have stalled or is not progressing as expected.

**Most importantly, if a child loses words they previously used or loses established communication, social, or motor skills, seek prompt medical evaluation.** Losing previously acquired skills should not be dismissed as a normal developmental stage.

## 7. Final Thoughts

Speech and language delays can have many causes, and not every child who talks late has the same underlying difficulty. The most important steps are to recognize potential signs early, understand the child's needs, and provide supportive opportunities for communication every day.

Parents can support language development through conversation, shared reading, interactive play, and responsive communication. However, home activities do not replace professional assessment when a child shows signs of developmental delay.

If you are concerned about your child's speech, talk to a pediatrician and ask about hearing, language, and overall developmental assessments. Timely support can help children develop communication skills and participate more fully in everyday life.
