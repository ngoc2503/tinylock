Constipation is common in children, particularly when they start eating solid foods or begin toilet training. A constipated child may have difficulty passing stool, hard or dry stools, abdominal discomfort, and fear of going to the toilet.

Parents sometimes assume that adding vegetables and giving more water will solve the problem. However, when constipation has lasted for a while or stool has built up in the bowel, dietary changes alone may not be enough.

**The key is to recognize constipation correctly, understand its possible causes, and choose an approach that is appropriate for the child's age.**

## 1. How Can You Tell If Your Child Is Constipated?

Not every child who goes several days without a bowel movement is constipated. Some babies, particularly breastfed babies, may pass stool less frequently while still having soft stools and appearing comfortable.

Parents should pay attention to stool consistency and the child's behavior.

Common signs include:

- Having fewer bowel movements than usual, sometimes fewer than three times a week.
- Passing hard, dry stools or small pellets that resemble rabbit droppings.
- Straining, struggling, or experiencing pain when passing stool.
- Crying, becoming fearful, or trying to hold stool in.
- Having a bloated abdomen, gas, or stomach pain that may improve after a bowel movement.
- Having a small amount of bright red blood on the stool or toilet paper because hard stool has caused a small anal fissure.
- In older children, stool leaking into their underwear despite being constipated.

Some children may stand on tiptoe, clench their buttocks, straighten their legs, or arch their backs to avoid passing stool. These behaviors can be signs that they are trying to hold it in because they are afraid of pain.

**Bowel movement frequency is only one factor. Hard stools, painful bowel movements, and withholding behavior are also important signs of constipation.**

## 2. Why Do Children Get Constipated?

Constipation can have several causes. Common contributing factors include:

### Not Getting Enough Fiber

Fiber helps increase stool bulk and supports normal bowel function. Children who eat few vegetables, fruits, or whole grains may be more likely to become constipated.

This can happen when:
- A child eats very few fruits and vegetables.
- Most meals consist of low-fiber foods.
- The child's diet lacks variety over a long period.

### Not Drinking Enough Fluids

When the body does not get enough fluids, stool may become dry and hard, making it more difficult to pass.

Fluid needs depend on age, diet, and health. Do not give extra water to babies under 6 months simply to treat constipation unless a healthcare professional advises it.

### Changes in Diet

Constipation may occur when a child:

- Starts eating solid foods.
- Transitions from breast milk to infant formula.
- Changes formula.
- Begins eating more solid foods.
- Experiences changes in eating habits or daily routines.

If constipation begins after a formula change, talk with a healthcare professional before repeatedly switching formulas.

### Withholding Stool

This is common in toddlers and children who are learning to use the toilet.

A child may avoid going to the toilet because:

- They previously experienced pain when passing stool.
- They do not want to stop playing.
- They feel uncomfortable using the school or public restroom.
- They are afraid of the toilet or are not yet comfortable using a potty.

When a child holds stool in, it stays in the bowel longer. More water is absorbed from it, making it harder. The next bowel movement may be even more painful, leading the child to hold stool in again. This creates a cycle that can make constipation worse.

### Limited Physical Activity

Children who spend long periods sitting or using screens may develop less regular bowel habits. Age-appropriate physical activity supports general digestive health.

### Other Medical Conditions

Less commonly, constipation may be associated with:

- Digestive system disorders.
- An underactive thyroid or metabolic conditions.
- Neurological conditions or structural abnormalities involving the anus or rectum.
- Side effects of certain medicines.
- Problems with nutrient absorption or nutrition.

If constipation begins at birth, persists for a long time, or occurs alongside unusual symptoms, a healthcare professional should assess the child for possible underlying causes.

## 3. What Should You Do If Your Child Is Constipated?

The right approach depends on the child's age, the severity of constipation, and their overall health.

### For Newborns and Babies Under 6 Months

Do not give plain water, fruit juice, honey, or laxatives to treat constipation without medical advice.

Parents can:

- Continue breastfeeding on demand.
- If the baby is formula-fed, prepare formula using the exact recommended proportions.
- Check whether the baby is feeding adequately and gaining weight appropriately.
- Contact a healthcare professional if the stool is hard, the baby appears to be in pain, or constipation persists.

Some breastfed babies may go several days between bowel movements while still passing soft stools, feeding well, and gaining weight normally. This does not necessarily mean they are constipated.

If constipation starts during the first few weeks of life, or the baby has abdominal swelling, vomiting, or other unusual symptoms, seek medical assessment promptly.

### For Babies Aged 6 Months and Older

Once a baby has started solid foods, parents can adjust meals to include more fiber-rich foods.

Suitable options may include:

- Vegetables such as leafy greens and broccoli.
- Fruits such as pears, prunes, peaches, papaya, and kiwi.
- Age-appropriate beans and legumes.
- Oats and suitable whole grains.

Offer water in amounts appropriate for the child's age, particularly when solid foods are introduced. Do not force excessive amounts of water or use water to replace milk feeds in young babies.

Increase fiber gradually to help avoid bloating and discomfort.

### For Children Aged 1 Year and Older

Parents can combine dietary changes, adequate fluids, and healthy toilet habits.

**1. Offer more fiber-rich foods**

Include vegetables, fruits, beans, and whole grains in daily meals. Offer a variety of foods rather than relying on just one fruit or vegetable.

**2. Provide enough fluids**

Offer water regularly throughout the day, especially after physical activity or in hot weather. Plain water is a suitable choice. Avoid using sugary drinks as a replacement for water.

**3. Establish a regular toilet routine**

- Encourage the child to sit on the potty or toilet after meals, especially after breakfast or dinner.
- Keep toilet sessions short and regular.
- Use a small footstool so the child's knees are slightly higher than their hips.
- Praise the child for cooperating, even if they do not pass stool.

**4. Encourage physical activity**

Encourage walking, running, outdoor play, cycling, or other age-appropriate activities.

**5. Keep the experience relaxed**

Do not force children to sit on the toilet for too long, scold them, or make them feel ashamed about accidents. Help them understand that using the toilet is a normal part of daily life.

## 4. When Does a Child Need Medicine for Constipation?

If constipation persists, stool builds up in the bowel, or dietary and lifestyle changes do not help, a healthcare professional may recommend a suitable laxative.

Some types of medicine used to treat constipation in children include:

- **Osmotic laxatives:** Help retain water in the bowel, making stool softer.
- **Stool softeners:** Help make stool easier to pass.
- **Stimulant laxatives:** May be considered by a healthcare professional in certain situations.

The choice of medicine, dosage, and treatment duration depend on the child's age, weight, and condition.

Parents should remember:

- Do not give children adult laxatives without medical advice.
- Do not use suppositories, enemas, or rectal treatments on your own.
- Do not stop prescribed treatment after just one bowel movement.
- Do not continue medication for a prolonged period without appropriate guidance and follow-up.

Some children need treatment for several weeks or months to maintain soft stools and restore regular bowel habits. Follow the treatment plan and attend follow-up appointments as advised.

## 5. When Should You Take Your Child to a Doctor?

Many cases of constipation can be managed with professional guidance and changes to daily habits. However, some symptoms may indicate a more serious problem.

**Seek emergency medical care if your child has:**

- Significant abdominal swelling together with vomiting.
- Severe or worsening abdominal pain.
- Green or yellow-green vomit.
- Inability to pass stool or gas together with abdominal swelling and pain.
- Extreme lethargy, marked weakness, or signs of severe dehydration.

**Arrange a medical assessment promptly if:**

- Constipation has been present since birth or during the first few weeks of life.
- A full-term newborn did not pass their first stool (meconium) within the first 48 hours after birth.
- Stools are persistently ribbon-shaped.
- The child is not gaining weight or growing as expected.
- Constipation keeps returning, persists, or does not improve with home care.
- There is blood in the stool, especially if it recurs or has no clear explanation.

If constipation lasts more than two weeks or does not improve with home care, arrange a medical assessment.

## 6. Common Mistakes Parents Should Avoid

1. **Focusing only on vegetables:** Fiber is important, but it may not be enough to resolve persistent constipation.
2. **Forcing a child to drink excessive amounts of water:** Provide age-appropriate fluids without forcing large quantities.
3. **Repeatedly changing infant formula:** Unnecessary formula changes can disrupt a child's feeding routine.
4. **Buying laxatives without medical advice:** Children need medicine and dosages suited to their individual condition.
5. **Scolding a child for withholding stool:** This may increase fear of using the toilet and make constipation worse.
6. **Stopping treatment too early:** Constipation may return if stool has not remained soft and regular bowel habits have not been restored.

## Conclusion

When a child is constipated, parents should observe stool consistency, bowel movement frequency, and the child's behavior to understand the problem. For children who have started solid foods, offer a varied diet, provide adequate fluids, encourage physical activity, and establish regular toilet habits.

If the child has significant pain, persistent constipation, blood in the stool, or other concerning symptoms, seek medical advice. Do not use laxatives or rectal treatments without appropriate guidance.

**A calm, consistent approach and treatment based on the underlying cause can help children develop healthier bowel habits and reduce the risk of constipation returning.**
