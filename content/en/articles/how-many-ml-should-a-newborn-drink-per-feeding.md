Newborns have very small stomachs, so they usually take small amounts of milk frequently during the first days of life. The right amount depends on the baby's age, weight, feeding method, and growth.

So, how many milliliters should a newborn drink at each feeding? How can parents tell whether their baby is getting enough milk, and when should they seek medical advice?

## 1. How much milk does a newborn need per feeding?

For a healthy, full-term baby who is bottle-fed expressed breast milk or infant formula, the following ranges can be used as general guidance.

| Age | Approximate Amount per Feeding | Typical Feeding Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| First day after birth | A very small amount, approximately 5–7 mL per feeding | Feed frequently in response to hunger cues |
| Day 2 | Approximately 14–15 mL per feeding | Usually 8–12 feeds in 24 hours |
| Day 3 | Approximately 22–27 mL per feeding | Approximately 8–12 feeds per day |
| Days 4–6 | Approximately 30 mL per feeding | Approximately 8–12 feeds per day |
| Day 7 | Approximately 30–60 mL per feeding as intake increases | Approximately 8–12 feeds per day |
| By the end of the first month | Many babies take approximately 90–120 mL per feeding | Usually every 3–4 hours, although some babies feed more frequently |

**Important:** These are general estimates, mainly for bottle-fed babies. Some babies need less or more depending on their individual needs. Do not force your baby to finish a bottle just to reach a specific amount.

## 2. What if the baby is directly breastfeeding?

During the first days after birth, the breasts produce colostrum—a small amount of milk that is rich in nutrients. Newborns often want to breastfeed very frequently, sometimes every 1–3 hours or in clusters.

Frequent feeding over a period of time does not automatically mean that the parent is producing too little milk. Look for signs that the baby latches well, sucks and swallows, and appears satisfied after some feeds.

If breastfeeding is painful, the baby struggles to latch, is unusually sleepy and difficult to wake, or is not gaining weight as expected, ask a doctor, midwife, or lactation consultant for help.

## 3. How can you tell whether your baby is getting enough milk?

Do not judge milk intake by milliliters alone. Consider several signs together:

- The baby has alert periods and shows early hunger cues, such as rooting or bringing their hands to their mouth.
- You can notice sucking and swallowing during feeds.
- When full, the baby may slow down, release the nipple, or turn away.
- Wet diapers increase over the first few days. By around day five, many babies who are feeding well have at least about six wet diapers a day.
- The baby's weight and growth are checked at routine appointments.

These signs should be considered together. If you are concerned about milk intake, contact a healthcare professional rather than independently increasing feeds or changing formula preparation.

## 4. Should you feed on a schedule or on demand?

In general, respond to early hunger cues, such as rooting, bringing hands to the mouth, lip-smacking, or mouth movements. Crying is often a later sign of hunger.

When bottle-feeding, feed slowly and watch your baby's responses. Do not pressure your baby to finish the prepared amount if they show signs of being full.

During the first weeks, some babies—especially those born prematurely or at a low birth weight, those with jaundice, or those who have not regained their birth weight—may need to be woken for feeds according to an individualized plan. Ask your baby's doctor or midwife whether waking is necessary and how often your baby should feed.

## 5. Safe bottle-feeding tips for newborns

- Prepare infant formula exactly as directed on the package. Do not add extra water or powder.
- Do not heat a bottle in a microwave, because hot spots can burn your baby's mouth.
- Do not pressure your baby to drink more after they show signs of fullness.
- Breast milk or infant formula is the main source of nutrition for newborns. Do not give extra water unless a healthcare professional advises it.
- Follow safe instructions for cleaning, storing, and using milk after preparation or after a feeding has begun.

## 6. When should you contact a healthcare professional?

Contact your baby's doctor or healthcare service promptly if your baby:

- Is feeding noticeably less or repeatedly refuses feeds.
- Is difficult to wake for feeding.
- Has fewer wet diapers than expected, a dry mouth, or signs of dehydration.
- Develops worsening jaundice.
- Is not gaining weight as expected.

Seek emergency medical help if your baby has trouble breathing, turns blue, becomes limp, is very difficult to wake, or appears seriously ill.

**Important:** A baby younger than 3 months with a measured temperature of **38°C (100.4°F) or higher** needs urgent medical assessment.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

### Should I make my baby drink 60 mL at every feeding?

Not necessarily. Intake varies between babies and from one feed to another. In the first days, a baby may take much less. Follow hunger and fullness cues, monitor wet diapers and weight gain, and follow advice from your healthcare professional.

### What if my baby still seems hungry after a feed?

If your baby continues to show hunger cues, you can offer more milk and watch their response. For bottle-feeding, feed slowly, allow pauses, and do not pressure the baby to finish the prepared amount.

### Should I wake my baby if they sleep for a long time?

It depends on the baby's age, weight, health, and whether they have regained their birth weight. During the first weeks, ask your doctor or midwife what to do if your baby sleeps through feeds. Premature babies, babies with jaundice, or babies who are not gaining weight well may need to be woken for feeds.

## Conclusion

Newborns generally take small amounts of milk frequently during the first days of life. For bottle-fed babies, around 30–60 mL per feed is a common guide during the first week as intake increases. By the end of the first month, many babies take about 90–120 mL per feed.

However, no single amount is right for every baby. Consider hunger and fullness cues, wet diapers, and weight gain together. If your baby is feeding poorly or you are concerned about milk intake, consult a healthcare professional.
