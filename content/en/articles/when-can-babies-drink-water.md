Many parents wonder when it is safe to give a baby plain water, especially during hot weather or when the baby begins eating solid foods. Offering water too early may reduce a baby's milk intake and can be harmful in some circumstances.

**Most healthy babies can begin having small sips of plain water at around 6 months of age, when solid foods are introduced. Before then, breast milk or infant formula usually provides all the fluid they need.**

## 1. At What Age Can Babies Drink Water?

### Babies aged 0–5 months

Babies younger than 6 months generally need only breast milk or infant formula to meet their nutritional and fluid needs.

- **Exclusively breastfed babies:** They do not need extra water, even in hot weather. If your baby seems thirsty or wants to feed more often, offer additional breastfeeding.
- **Formula-fed babies:** Continue feeding infant formula according to age-appropriate guidance. Do not routinely offer extra plain water unless a healthcare professional advises you to.
- **Never dilute formula beyond the instructions:** Always use the exact water-to-powder ratio stated on the packaging. Extra water can reduce nutritional intake and disturb the baby's electrolyte balance.

Giving a baby under 6 months too much water may reduce milk intake and, in serious cases, contribute to electrolyte disturbances, including low blood sodium.

### Babies aged 6–11 months

At around 6 months, when complementary foods are introduced, parents can begin offering small sips of plain water during or after meals.

At this stage:

- Start with small amounts and gradually adjust to your baby's needs and drinking skills.
- Offer water in a small open cup, training cup, or suitable straw cup.
- Continue regular breastfeeding or infant formula feeds.
- Do not force your baby to finish a fixed amount of water.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) suggests that babies aged 6–12 months may be offered approximately 4–8 ounces (120–240 mL) of water per day. This is a general guide, not a mandatory target for every baby. Actual needs vary with milk intake, food, weather, and health.

**Important:** During the first year, breast milk or infant formula remains the main source of nutrition and fluid. Water should not replace regular milk feeds.

### Children aged 12 months and older

After the first birthday, plain water can become a regular drink throughout the day.

Parents can:

- Offer water when the child is thirsty and at meals, after active play, or during hot weather.
- Make safe drinking water readily available.
- Encourage plain water instead of sugary drinks.
- Limit sweetened beverages and other drinks high in added sugar.
- Continue offering a varied, age-appropriate diet.

A child's daily fluid needs depend on age, body size, diet, physical activity, and weather. There is no single amount that is right for every child.

## 2. Should Babies Under 6 Months Drink Water in Hot Weather?

This is a common misconception in infant care.

The World Health Organization (WHO) advises that exclusively breastfed babies under 6 months do not need extra water, even in hot weather. Breast milk provides the water they need.

Instead of offering plain water, parents can:

- Breastfeed more frequently.
- Prepare infant formula exactly as directed.
- Keep the baby's environment comfortably cool.
- Monitor feeding, wet diapers, and alertness.

If your baby shows signs of dehydration, has a fever, or feeds poorly, seek advice from a healthcare professional rather than trying to manage the problem by giving extra water.

## 3. Should Babies Drink Water When They Have a Fever or Diarrhea?

The appropriate way to replace fluids depends on the child's age and illness.

### When a baby has a fever

- For babies under 6 months, continue breastfeeding or offering correctly prepared infant formula.
- From around 6 months, small sips of water may be offered alongside milk and age-appropriate foods.
- Seek medical advice if your baby feeds poorly, urinates less than usual, becomes unusually sleepy, or shows signs of dehydration.

### When a child has diarrhea or vomiting

Children can lose both fluids and electrolytes. Depending on their condition, an **oral rehydration solution (ORS)** may be recommended.

Prepare ORS exactly as directed on the package. Do not change the dilution or mix it with milk, juice, or other drinks unless instructed by a healthcare professional.

Giving large amounts of plain water alone is not an appropriate substitute for treating significant fluid loss from vomiting or diarrhea. Infants and young children who are ill may need assessment based on their age and degree of dehydration.

Seek urgent medical care if your child vomits repeatedly and cannot keep fluids down, urinates much less than usual, is difficult to wake, or has signs of severe dehydration.

## 4. What Type of Water Is Suitable for Babies?

Once your baby is old enough to drink water, consider the following:

- **Safe drinking water:** Use water that meets local safety standards. If its safety is uncertain, treat it appropriately before use.
- **Plain, unsweetened water:** This is a suitable choice for helping babies learn to drink water.
- **An age-appropriate cup:** Choose a safe cup that your baby can handle according to their developmental abilities.

Do not give babies herbal drinks, leaf infusions, sugar water, or products marketed to improve sleep or digestion without appropriate medical guidance.

Fruit juice should not be a regular drink for babies under 12 months. Breast milk or infant formula should continue throughout the first year.

## 5. What Are the Signs of Dehydration?

Parents should watch for:

- Noticeably fewer wet diapers or less frequent urination.
- A dry mouth or lips.
- Fewer tears when crying.
- Feeding or drinking less than usual.
- Unusual tiredness, reduced activity, or difficulty waking.
- A sunken soft spot on an infant's head.

These signs do not always appear together. If your child shows clear signs of dehydration, especially alongside fever, vomiting, or diarrhea, seek medical assessment promptly.

A baby younger than 3 months with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher needs urgent medical assessment, even if there are no obvious signs of dehydration.

## 6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Giving water to babies under 6 months as a routine practice:** Healthy babies generally do not need extra water beyond breast milk or infant formula.
2. **Using water as a substitute for milk:** This can reduce the nutrients a baby receives.
3. **Diluting infant formula:** Extra water can cause inadequate nutrition and electrolyte disturbances.
4. **Forcing a child to drink a large amount:** Offer water according to age and ability rather than insisting on a fixed quantity.
5. **Treating dehydration with plain water alone:** Vomiting and diarrhea may require an appropriate rehydration plan and professional guidance.

## Conclusion

Most healthy babies can begin having small sips of plain water at around **6 months of age**, when they start eating solid foods. Before 6 months, breast milk or infant formula usually provides the fluid a baby needs, so extra water should not be given routinely.

If your child has a fever, vomiting, diarrhea, or signs of dehydration, pay attention to their overall condition and seek appropriate medical advice rather than simply increasing water intake.
