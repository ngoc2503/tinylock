A child who sleeps for long periods during the day but stays awake, cries, or wants to play at night can leave parents exhausted. This pattern is often described as **day-night reversal**. It is common in newborns because their body clocks are still developing. In older children, long or late daytime naps and inconsistent daily routines may also make it harder to fall asleep at night.

How can parents help their child develop a healthier sleep routine without interfering with the rest their growing body needs?

## 1. Why does a child sleep more during the day and stay awake at night?

Several factors can contribute to an irregular sleep schedule.

### An immature body clock

Newborns do not yet have a well-established sense of day and night. During the first weeks of life, they sleep in short or long stretches, wake frequently to feed, and may be alert at night. Their sleep-wake rhythm gradually develops as they grow and experience regular light-dark cycles.

### Long or late daytime naps

For older babies and children, a long nap or one that ends close to bedtime may reduce their need for sleep at night. They may not feel sleepy at the usual bedtime or may stay awake later than expected.

### An inconsistent daily routine

Frequently changing wake-up times, mealtimes, naps, and bedtimes can make it harder for a child's body to develop a regular rhythm. Sleeping much later on weekends can also disrupt the schedule.

### Bright light and screen use in the evening

Bright light and stimulating activities before bedtime can make it harder for children to wind down. Watching television or using a phone or tablet close to bedtime may also interfere with the transition to sleep.

### Developmental changes or physical discomfort

Teething, illness, a blocked nose, hunger, changes in the sleep environment, or separation anxiety may cause more frequent night waking. Sleep patterns can temporarily change during these periods.

## 2. How much sleep does a child need?

Before trying to reduce daytime sleep, consider the child's total sleep over 24 hours. Daytime naps are still important for young children and should not automatically be treated as a problem.

The following figures are general guidelines, not strict targets for every child:

| Age | Approximate total sleep in 24 hours |
|---|---:|
| Newborns | Around 14–17 hours, with considerable variation |
| 3–6 months | Around 12–16 hours |
| 6–12 months | Around 12–16 hours |
| 1–2 years | Around 11–14 hours |
| 3–5 years | Around 10–13 hours |
| 6–12 years | Around 9–12 hours |

These totals include daytime naps. Individual sleep needs vary.

**Important:** Newborns and young babies may still need to wake for night feeds. Do not force a baby to sleep through the night or remove night feeds simply to change their sleep schedule.

## 3. Help your child learn the difference between day and night

### During the day: Provide natural light and age-appropriate activity

- Open the curtains so your child gets natural daylight.
- Offer age-appropriate activities such as talking, playing, reading, or gentle movement.
- Keep normal household sounds during daytime naps; the home does not need to be completely silent.
- Allow your child to nap when they show signs of sleepiness rather than keeping them awake for too long.

For newborns, gently distinguish daytime from nighttime while still meeting their needs for sleep and feeding.

### At night: Reduce light and stimulation

- Dim the lights as bedtime approaches.
- Keep noise and active play to a minimum.
- When your child wakes for a feed or diaper change, keep the interaction calm, speak softly, and use only as much light as needed.
- Once their needs have been met, gently help them return to sleep.
- Avoid turning on the television or using a phone to entertain a child who wakes at night.

A consistent contrast between daytime and nighttime environments can gradually help children recognize when it is time to be active and when it is time to rest.

## 4. Adjust daytime sleep according to age

Children of different ages need different approaches. A single sleep schedule will not suit everyone.

### Newborns under 3 months

At this stage, babies do not yet have a stable day-night rhythm. Parents can:

- Feed the baby responsively and follow newborn-care guidance.
- Keep daytime surroundings bright and nighttime surroundings quiet and dim.
- Watch for sleep cues such as yawning, rubbing the eyes, looking away, or mild fussiness.
- Avoid repeatedly waking the baby or deliberately keeping them awake longer in the hope that they will sleep more at night.

If your baby was born prematurely, has a low birth weight, jaundice, or weight-gain concerns, ask a healthcare professional whether you need to wake the baby for feeds.

### Babies aged 3–6 months

Sleep patterns may become more predictable, but babies still need several periods of sleep during the day.

- Keep wake-up times and daily routines reasonably consistent.
- Offer naps when your baby shows signs of tiredness; avoid allowing them to become overtired.
- Establish a calming bedtime routine, such as changing the diaper, dimming the lights, singing, or gentle cuddling.
- If long daytime naps are repeatedly making bedtime difficult, discuss an age-appropriate schedule with a healthcare professional.

### Babies aged 6–12 months

Many babies gradually develop a more predictable daytime nap pattern.

- Maintain daytime naps that meet your baby's needs.
- If a late nap regularly makes bedtime difficult, consider adjusting its timing.
- Encourage age-appropriate play and exposure to natural daylight.
- Follow the same calming bedtime routine each evening.

Babies may still wake at night because of hunger, teething, discomfort, or the need for reassurance. This does not necessarily mean their sleep routine is wrong.

### Children aged 1–5 years

At this age, daytime naps may have a more noticeable effect on nighttime sleep.

- Keep wake-up times reasonably consistent.
- If your child still needs a nap, schedule it earlier or in the middle of the afternoon.
- Avoid naps that are too late or too long if they regularly interfere with bedtime.
- If your child is gradually outgrowing naps, offer quiet rest time instead.
- Establish a bedtime routine that may include bathing, brushing teeth, reading, and turning off the lights.

Do not suddenly remove naps if your child still appears tired and needs daytime rest.

### Children aged 6–12 years

School-age children need a consistent sleep schedule to support attention, learning, and daily activities.

- Keep bedtime and wake-up times reasonably consistent, including on weekends.
- Avoid long daytime naps to make up for insufficient nighttime sleep.
- Encourage physical activity and outdoor time.
- Avoid tea, coffee, energy drinks, and other sources of caffeine.
- Turn off electronic devices around 30–60 minutes before bedtime.
- Plan homework and recreational activities so your child does not regularly stay up too late.

## 5. Build a healthy bedtime routine

A simple routine repeated every day can help children recognize that it is time to wind down.

Parents can try this sequence:

1. Finish the meal or age-appropriate milk feed.
2. Bathe or complete personal hygiene.
3. Change into sleepwear and brush teeth if age-appropriate.
4. Dim the lights and put away electronic devices.
5. Read a story, sing a lullaby, or talk quietly.
6. Put the child to bed at an age-appropriate time.

For older children, allow around 20–30 minutes for a calming routine before sleep. If your child regularly goes to bed too late, move bedtime earlier in small steps rather than making a sudden change.

## 6. Common mistakes parents should avoid

- **Keeping a child awake all day:** An overtired child may become irritable and find it harder to fall asleep.
- **Removing daytime naps completely:** Young children still need daytime sleep according to their age and individual needs.
- **Frequently waking newborns to change their schedule:** This may interfere with their need for rest and feeding.
- **Using phones or television to settle a child:** Light and stimulating content can make it harder to wind down.
- **Giving sleeping medication or melatonin without medical advice:** Do not use sleep medicines or supplements for a child unless advised by a healthcare professional.
- **Comparing your child's sleep with other children's routines:** Sleep needs and the development of sleep rhythms vary from child to child.

## 7. When should you seek medical advice?

Daytime sleep and nighttime waking may be related to routine, but parents should seek medical advice if they notice:

- Unusual sleepiness, difficulty waking, or reduced responsiveness.
- Poor feeding, refusing food, fewer wet diapers or urination, or signs of dehydration.
- Frequent loud snoring, difficulty breathing, or pauses in breathing during sleep.
- Repeated waking accompanied by pain, discomfort, or other signs of illness.
- Persistent tiredness or daytime sleepiness that affects school, play, or daily activities.
- A disrupted sleep schedule that continues despite a consistent routine.

If your child has difficulty breathing, turns blue, stops breathing, or is unusually difficult to wake, seek emergency medical care immediately.

## 8. Advice for parents

A child who sleeps more during the day and stays awake at night does not necessarily have a sleep problem. In newborns, this pattern is often part of the gradual development of their body clock. As children grow, daytime naps, light exposure, daily activities, and bedtime habits can all influence nighttime sleep.

Make changes gradually and choose routines that match your child's age and sleep needs. The goal is not to force a child to sleep at a particular time at all costs, but to help them get enough rest and develop healthy sleep habits.
