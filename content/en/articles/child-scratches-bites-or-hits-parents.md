**Not necessarily a sign of a problem.** Young children, particularly those around 1–3 years old, may sometimes scratch, bite, hit, or pull hair as they explore their surroundings, learn to communicate, and develop emotional regulation. A child who cannot yet express anger, frustration, or needs in words may use physical actions instead.

However, this does not mean parents should ignore the behavior. It is important to understand why it happens, teach safer ways to express feelings, and recognize when professional advice may be needed.

## 1. Why Do Children Scratch, Bite, or Hit Their Parents?

These behaviors can have different causes. Parents should not immediately assume that a child is naughty, disrespectful, or deliberately trying to cause pain.

### Children cannot yet express their feelings in words

This is a common reason among young children. When children have limited language skills and are still learning to regulate emotions, they may scratch, bite, or hit when they:

- Cannot have something they want.
- Are stopped from doing a favorite activity.
- Do not want an adult to take away a toy.
- Feel angry, frustrated, or uncomfortable.
- Want to communicate that they dislike something.

For example, when a parent turns off a phone or refuses a request, a child may hit because they do not yet know how to say, “I'm angry” or “I want to keep playing.”

### Children want attention

Some children notice that biting or hitting immediately makes their parents react: shouting, jumping in surprise, talking at length, or chasing them.

They may repeat the behavior because it gets a strong reaction, even though they do not fully understand that it hurts someone.

### Children are exploring and testing reactions

Young children, especially those who are still learning social rules, may scratch, bite, or hit out of curiosity:

- They want to see how adults respond.
- They do not yet understand that the action causes pain.
- They imitate behavior they have seen.
- They have not yet learned the difference between playful contact and hurting someone.

This does not necessarily mean they have harmful intentions. However, parents should still set clear limits.

### Children are tired, hungry, or overstimulated

Children may find it harder to control their behavior when they are sleepy, hungry, uncomfortable, overwhelmed by noise, or involved in too many activities.

Some children may also bite while teething, when they feel physically uncomfortable, or when they are adjusting to changes in their routine.

### Children imitate behavior around them

Children learn a great deal by observing others. If they frequently see people hitting, scratching, biting, or using force to resolve disagreements, they may imitate those actions.

Violent or age-inappropriate content on television, phones, or games can also provide behavior for children to observe and copy.

## 2. Is This Behavior Normal at Different Ages?

Children's understanding and ability to control their behavior change as they grow. Parents should consider the child's age and developmental stage.

| Age | Behaviors that may occur |
|---|---|
| Under 1 year | Babies may explore objects with their mouths, bite while teething, or accidentally hurt adults during play. Their understanding of rules is very limited. |
| 1–2 years | Children may bite, hit, or scratch when angry, frustrated, or unable to express their needs in words. These behaviors are relatively common at this stage. |
| 2–3 years | Children begin to understand some rules, but emotional regulation is still developing. Hitting or biting may occur when they do not get their way. |
| 3–4 years | Children generally develop better communication and emotional regulation. If hurtful behavior happens frequently, parents should look more closely at possible causes. |
| 5 years and older | Children generally have a better understanding that scratching, biting, and hitting hurt others. Repeated, severe, or relationship-disrupting behavior deserves closer assessment. |

**Important:** These are general developmental descriptions, not diagnostic milestones. Children differ in temperament, language skills, and developmental pace. Frequency, severity, context, and impact matter—not age alone.

## 3. What Should Parents Do When a Child Scratches, Bites, or Hits Them?

The goal is not to frighten the child. It is to help them understand boundaries and learn safer ways to express emotions.

### Step 1: Stop the behavior immediately and calmly

When a child hits, scratches, or bites, gently block their hands, create some distance, or move them away from the situation if necessary.

Use a calm, firm voice:

- “No hitting. Hitting hurts Mommy.”
- “You must not bite Daddy.”
- “I won't let you scratch people.”

Do not laugh, shout excessively, or hit the child back.

### Step 2: Name the child's feelings

Once the behavior has stopped, help the child understand what they may be feeling.

For example:

- “You're angry because I turned off the phone.”
- “You're upset because Dad took your toy, aren't you?”
- “You're frustrated because playtime has to end.”

Naming emotions helps children gradually connect their feelings with words instead of using physical actions to communicate.

### Step 3: Teach an alternative behavior

Do not simply say, “Don't hit.” Show the child what they can do instead.

| When the child wants to… | Teach the child to… |
|---|---|
| Express anger | Say, “I'm angry.” |
| Disagree | Say, “I don't like that.” |
| Get a parent's attention | Call the parent or gently touch their arm. |
| Ask for an object | Say, “Can I have it?” or “Is it my turn?” |
| Release strong feelings | Squeeze a soft ball, hug a pillow, or do a safe physical activity. |

For children who cannot speak yet, parents can use gestures, pictures, or simple words.

### Step 4: Praise the child when they manage their behavior

When a child feels angry but stops themselves, uses words, or gently touches someone instead of hitting, acknowledge it:

- “You told me you were angry. Well done.”
- “I'm glad you called me instead of hitting.”
- “You used your words to ask for the toy.”

Specific praise helps children understand which behaviors are encouraged and motivates them to repeat positive actions.

### Step 5: Observe patterns and identify triggers

Parents can keep track of a few details:

- When does the child usually scratch, bite, or hit?
- Does it happen when the child is refused something, hungry, or sleepy?
- Does the child do it to everyone or only to parents?
- How do adults usually respond afterward?
- Have there been recent changes in the child's environment, caregivers, or routine?

Identifying triggers helps parents prevent situations before the behavior occurs instead of only reacting after a child has hit or bitten someone.

## 4. Common Parenting Responses to Avoid

Some reactions may seem memorable, but they can make the behavior harder to manage.

- **Hitting or scratching the child back:** The child may learn that physical force is an acceptable way to respond when angry.
- **Biting the child to show them how it feels:** This does not teach self-control and may frighten the child or encourage imitation.
- **Shouting, threatening, or humiliating the child:** The child may become more distressed without learning how to express feelings appropriately.
- **Laughing when the child hits:** The child may interpret the behavior as a game and repeat it.
- **Calling the child naughty, stubborn, or aggressive:** Labels do not address the cause and may affect how adults treat the child.
- **Only forbidding the behavior without teaching an alternative:** Children need a specific replacement behavior suited to their age and communication skills.

## 5. When Should Parents Seek Professional Advice?

Although scratching, biting, or hitting can occur during development, parents should speak with a pediatrician or child mental health professional if they notice that:

- The child frequently hits, bites, or scratches others with considerable force.
- The behavior persists, becomes more severe, or does not improve despite consistent guidance.
- The child injures parents, siblings, or other children.
- The child has difficulty managing emotions and frequently has intense outbursts.
- The behavior occurs alongside other difficulties with communication, language, social interaction, or development.
- The child shows signs of anxiety, fear, a significant change in behavior, or possible exposure to violence.

If the child poses a risk to themselves or others, prioritize everyone's safety and seek professional support promptly. There is no need to wait until the behavior becomes severe before asking for help.

Scratching, biting, or hitting does not automatically mean that a child has a behavioral disorder, autism, or another developmental condition. Only a qualified professional can assess this by considering the child's overall development and behavior.

## 6. How Can Parents Prevent Scratching, Biting, and Hitting?

Parents can create an environment that helps children learn emotional regulation from an early age:

1. **Maintain suitable daily routines:** Make sure the child gets enough sleep, eats regularly, and has time to play.
2. **Spend time interacting positively:** Read, talk, and play together so the child receives positive attention.
3. **Model peaceful conflict resolution:** Adults should use calm words instead of hitting, shouting, or threatening.
4. **Choose age-appropriate content:** Limit exposure to violent or unsuitable media.
5. **Keep responses consistent among caregivers:** Parents and other caregivers should follow similar rules so the child receives clear messages.
6. **Give children opportunities to practice:** Use play to teach saying no, asking for help, waiting for a turn, and expressing feelings.

## 7. A Final Word for Parents

When a child scratches, bites, or hits, parents may feel hurt, angry, or disappointed. However, remember that young children are still learning to understand their emotions and control their behavior.

The key is to **accept the child's feelings without accepting behavior that hurts others**. Parents can say: “You are allowed to feel angry, but you are not allowed to hit Mommy.”

Be patient, set consistent boundaries, model appropriate behavior, and teach practical alternatives. If the behavior is frequent, severe, or accompanied by other developmental concerns, seek professional guidance.
