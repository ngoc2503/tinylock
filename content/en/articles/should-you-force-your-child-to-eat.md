When children eat very little, hold food in their mouths, turn their heads away, or refuse meals, many parents worry that they are not getting enough nutrients and may not grow properly. As a result, some parents try to feed extra bites, insist that children finish everything on their plates, use videos to distract them, or even scold them when they refuse food.

However, **parents should not force children to eat when they do not want to**. Pressure during mealtimes can make children anxious, create negative associations with food, and make future meals more difficult.

Instead of forcing children to eat, parents should understand why they are refusing food, establish a suitable meal routine, and create a positive eating environment. What should you do when your child refuses to eat?

## 1. Should you force your child to eat when they don't want to?

The answer is **no**.

Children can recognize hunger and fullness, but their food intake may vary from day to day depending on their activity level, sleep, stage of development, and health.

Some days children have a good appetite, while on other days they may eat only a small amount. This does not necessarily mean they are malnourished.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends responsive feeding. This means caregivers recognize and respond appropriately to a child's hunger and fullness cues and developmental abilities. Children should be encouraged patiently, but never forced to eat.

Parents can decide:
- What foods are offered.
- When meals and snacks are served.
- Where meals take place and what the eating environment is like.

Children should be encouraged to decide:
- Whether to eat from the foods offered.
- Which foods to choose from the available options.
- How much to eat according to their hunger and fullness.

This does not mean allowing children to eat anything at any time. Parents still need to provide suitable foods, maintain a routine, and set reasonable boundaries.

## 2. Why should you avoid forcing children to eat?

Forcing children to eat can have several unwanted effects:

**1. It can make children afraid of mealtimes**

If every meal involves scolding, threats, or being made to sit until the plate is empty, children may become anxious whenever it is time to eat.

**2. It can interfere with recognizing hunger and fullness**

When children are repeatedly told to eat more even after they feel full, they may pay less attention to their body's natural signals. Over time, this can make it harder to develop healthy self-regulation of food intake.

**3. It can increase resistance during meals**

Young children are developing independence. Constantly being told to eat according to an adult's wishes may lead them to hold food in their mouths, turn away, cry, or refuse meals.

**4. It can reduce interest in trying new foods**

Children need time to become familiar with the smell, color, texture, and taste of food. Forcing them to eat unfamiliar foods immediately may make them more reluctant to try those foods again.

**5. It can create a negative relationship with food**

When food is repeatedly used as a reward, punishment, or bargaining tool, children may learn to see eating as a struggle rather than a natural need.

Not every child who has been pressured to eat will develop these problems. However, avoiding pressure is a better way to support positive eating habits.

## 3. Why might a child not want to eat?

Before trying to improve a child's appetite, parents should consider possible reasons for food refusal.

### Physiological and developmental reasons

- **Changing appetite:** Appetite and energy needs are not the same every day.
- **Slower growth:** After the rapid growth of the first year, some children naturally eat less than they did before.
- **Developmental feeding stages:** Children begin seeking independence, exploring their surroundings, and becoming easily distracted during meals.
- **Fear of unfamiliar foods:** Some children, particularly around 18 months to 2 years, begin refusing unfamiliar foods. This is often called food neophobia.

### Lifestyle and eating habits

- Too many sweets, milk drinks, or snacks before meals.
- Drinking too much juice, sweetened drinks, or milk, which can make children feel full.
- Irregular meal times or eating continuously throughout the day.
- Insufficient sleep, tiredness, or distractions from phones and television.
- Portions that are too large and overwhelming.

### Health-related reasons

Children may eat less because of:
- A sore throat, mouth ulcers, oral inflammation, or teething discomfort.
- Constipation, abdominal pain, reflux, or digestive problems.
- Fever, flu, or other infections.
- Food allergies or intolerances.
- Difficulty chewing or swallowing, or sensitivity to certain food textures.
- Other health or developmental conditions.

If a child suddenly stops eating, appears to be in pain, or shows changes in health, parents should not assume it is simply a normal appetite phase.

## 4. What should parents do when their child refuses to eat?

### Step 1: Stay calm and avoid turning meals into a battle

When a child turns away, holds food in their mouth, or refuses a meal, parents should remain calm.

Avoid:
- Shouting, threatening, or hitting the child.
- Forcing the child's mouth open or pushing food into their mouth when they resist.
- Making the child sit for a long time until the plate is empty.
- Comparing the child with siblings or other children.
- Using pressure such as, "Mom won't love you if you don't eat," or "You must finish everything before you can play."

Gently end the meal when your child clearly shows that they are full or no longer want to eat.

### Step 2: Establish a consistent eating routine

Try to keep meal times reasonably consistent and appropriate for your child's age and needs.

For children who have started complementary feeding, general WHO guidance includes:
- 6–8 months: about 2–3 complementary meals per day.
- 9–23 months: about 3–4 complementary meals per day, with 1–2 snacks if needed.

For older children, parents can plan three main meals and one or two nutritious snacks, depending on age, portion needs, and activity level.

Avoid continuous snacking or giving too much milk immediately before meals. However, do not deliberately make children excessively hungry to force them to eat.

### Step 3: Start with small portions

A plate filled with too much food may make children feel overwhelmed.

Parents can:
- Serve a small, age-appropriate portion.
- Include familiar foods that the child already enjoys.
- Offer a small amount of a new food for exploration.
- Offer more if the child is still hungry and wants it.

Children do not need to finish everything on their plates.

### Step 4: Encourage children to explore food

Children do not need to eat a new food the first time they see it.

Parents can let children:
- Look at the food's colors and shapes.
- Touch, hold, smell, or taste a very small amount.
- Help wash vegetables, pick leaves, or prepare food in age-appropriate ways.
- Choose between two healthy options prepared by their parents.

Patiently offer the food again on other occasions. You can change how it is prepared or presented. Children may need repeated exposure before accepting a particular food.

### Step 5: Eat together and set a good example

Children learn eating habits by observing adults.

Parents should:
- Sit and eat with their children whenever possible.
- Eat a variety of foods and show enjoyment of healthy meals.
- Have pleasant conversations instead of focusing only on how much the child eats.
- Encourage children to feed themselves when they are ready, while ensuring that food is safe for their age and abilities.

### Step 6: Limit electronic devices during meals

Avoid making phones, television, or videos a routine way to get children to open their mouths and eat passively.

When children focus on screens, they may pay less attention to food and their body's hunger and fullness signals.

Create a calm eating environment and limit toys and electronic devices so children can focus on their meals.

### Step 7: Monitor overall food intake and growth

Do not assess your child's nutrition based on a single meal or one day of eating.

Observe:
- Food intake and dietary variety over the week.
- Your child's activity level and mood.
- Weight and height over time.
- Urination, bowel movements, and other signs of health.

If your child is happy, active, and growing appropriately, occasional days of eating less are generally less concerning.

## 5. What should you do when your child refuses certain foods?

**Your child refuses vegetables:** Try different preparations, such as steaming them until soft, adding them to soup, chopping them finely, or serving them with familiar foods. Do not force your child to eat a large portion of vegetables immediately.

**Your child only likes a few foods:** Continue offering familiar favorites alongside other nutritious foods. Expand the menu gradually rather than preparing only one preferred food at every meal.

**Your child refuses to try new foods:** Place a small amount of the new food next to familiar foods. Give your child time to look at and explore it without requiring them to taste it immediately.

**Your child holds food in their mouth for a long time:** Check whether the food is too hard, chewy, dry, or unsuitable for their chewing and swallowing abilities. If this happens frequently, their feeding and swallowing skills should be assessed.

**Your child only eats while watching a phone:** Gradually rebuild a screen-free eating routine. Start with short meals, reduce distractions, and eat together with your child.

## 6. When should you seek medical advice for poor appetite?

Have your child assessed by a healthcare professional if you notice any of the following:

- Weight gain has stopped, weight is decreasing, or the child is dropping from their previous growth trajectory.
- The child eats very little for a prolonged period or continues to restrict the range of foods they accept.
- The child is tired, less active, unusually pale, or shows signs of nutritional deficiency.
- Frequent vomiting, abdominal pain, constipation, or diarrhea.
- Coughing, choking, gagging, or difficulty chewing or swallowing during meals.
- Mealtimes are consistently prolonged, stressful, or associated with clear signs of fear.
- The child accepts only a very limited range of foods and this affects health, growth, or daily activities.

Some children may need further assessment of nutrition, digestion, chewing and swallowing skills, development, or feeding behavior. Parents should not diagnose an eating disorder based only on picky eating.

**Seek emergency medical help immediately** if your child shows signs of severe dehydration, becomes unusually drowsy or difficult to wake, has difficulty breathing, chokes, or cannot swallow. In young infants, especially those under 6 months, a significant reduction in feeding or refusal to breastfeed or take formula should be assessed promptly.

## 7. Common mistakes parents should avoid

- Forcing children to finish their portions even when they are full.
- Threatening, shouting at, or punishing children for refusing food.
- Using sweets, toys, or phones as rewards for every bite.
- Allowing constant snacking that reduces appetite at main meals.
- Preparing only the foods children like and stopping the introduction of new foods.
- Giving appetite stimulants, digestive supplements, or nutritional products without appropriate medical advice.
- Judging nutrition only by how much a child eats at one meal.

## 8. Frequently asked questions

**Should I worry if my child skips one meal?**

If your child is alert, drinking enough, behaving normally, and showing no signs of illness, occasionally skipping one meal may not be a concern. Continue the usual eating routine and do not force the child to make up for the missed meal. Seek medical advice if food refusal persists or other symptoms appear.

**My child eats very little but is still gaining weight. Should I force them to eat?**

No. Do not force a child to eat simply because the portion looks small to you. Consider growth over time, activity level, and dietary variety. If the child is developing appropriately, continue to support positive eating habits.

**Should I reward my child with sweets for finishing a meal?**

It is better not to use sweets as a regular reward. This may make children value sweets more than other foods. Instead, praise their effort, enjoy family activities, or offer opportunities for play.

**Should I give my child milk instead of a meal when they refuse food?**

Do not automatically replace every meal with milk. Drinking too much milk can make children feel full and reduce their interest in solid foods. Milk needs depend on age, nutritional status, and the child's overall diet. Children under 12 months still need breast milk or appropriate infant formula as their main source of nutrition, but they also need age-appropriate complementary foods once they reach the recommended age.

**How can I tell whether my child is eating enough?**

Do not rely only on how much your child eats at one meal. Monitor growth, weight, height, activity, general health, and dietary variety over time. A doctor or dietitian can provide an individual assessment if you are concerned.

## Conclusion

Parents should not force children to eat when they do not want to. Instead, establish a suitable meal routine, offer a variety of foods, start with small portions, give children opportunities to explore food, and respect their hunger and fullness cues.

The goal is not to make children finish a plate at every meal. It is to help them develop healthy eating habits, feel comfortable with food, and grow well over time.
