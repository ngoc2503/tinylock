Tantrums are a normal part of growing up and are most common between about 1 and 4 years old. They are not a sign that your child is "bad" or that you are doing something wrong.

## Why tantrums happen

At this age children feel big emotions but do not yet have the words to express them or the self-control to manage them. Common triggers:

- Wanting something and being told no
- Being tired, hungry or sleepy
- Being pulled suddenly from something they enjoy
- Wanting to do something on their own but not being able to

## During a tantrum

1. **Stay calm.** Children learn to calm down from how you respond.
2. **Keep them safe.** Move your child away from danger and clear anything they could hit.
3. **Name the feeling.** For example: "You're really angry because TV time is over."
4. **Hold reasonable decisions.** If you give in as soon as your child cries or screams, they learn that tantrums work.
5. **Stay close**, so your child knows you are there, but skip the explanations while they cannot yet listen.

> When your child is out of control, it is not the moment to teach a lesson. Wait until they have calmed down.

## After your child settles

Give a hug, briefly say what happened and what they could do differently next time. Avoid scolding or going over it for too long.

## Ways to reduce tantrums

- Keep mealtimes and bedtimes regular
- Give a heads-up before changing activities ("Five more minutes, then we'll tidy up")
- Offer small choices ("Do you want the blue shirt or the yellow one?")
- Praise your child when they calm themselves or use words to say what they want

## When to talk to a doctor

Talk to your pediatrician or a child psychologist if:

- Tantrums are very frequent, long-lasting and hard to soothe
- Your child hurts themselves or others
- Your child holds their breath until they turn blue or faint
- Your child is older than 4-5 and still has frequent, intense tantrums
- Your child loses skills they already had (speech, toilet use...) or you are worried about their development
