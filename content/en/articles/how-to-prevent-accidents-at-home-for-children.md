Home is where children play, explore, and spend much of their time. However, everyday household items such as stairs, electrical outlets, kitchen appliances, medicines, cleaning products, and bathtubs can become dangerous if they are not properly secured.

Young children are particularly vulnerable because they are naturally curious but do not yet fully understand danger. Common household accidents include falls, burns, choking, poisoning, electric shocks, and drowning.

So, how can parents prevent accidents at home? The key is to remove potential hazards, create a safer living environment, and supervise children according to their age and developmental abilities.

## 1. Common household accidents in children

Children may experience accidents during ordinary daily activities:

- **Falls:** Falling from stairs, beds, chairs, balconies, windows, or slipping on wet floors.
- **Burns and scalds:** Touching hot pots, kettles, irons, stoves, hot water, or freshly cooked food.
- **Choking and suffocation:** Swallowing unsafe foods, small toys, coins, button batteries, or becoming tangled in curtain cords or plastic bags.
- **Poisoning:** Accidentally swallowing medicines, cleaning products, pesticides, or chemicals stored in incorrectly labeled containers.
- **Electric shocks:** Touching electrical outlets, exposed wires, or damaged appliances.
- **Drowning:** Getting into danger in bathtubs, buckets, basins, swimming pools, or other places containing water.
- **Furniture tip-overs:** Being struck or crushed by unsecured cabinets, shelves, televisions, tables, or other heavy furniture.

Many of these risks can be significantly reduced by making the home safer and providing appropriate supervision.

## 2. How to prevent accidents in different areas of the home

### 2.1. Living room and bedroom: Prevent falls, collisions, and suffocation

These are areas where children often play and sleep. Parents should:

- Install safety gates at the top and bottom of stairs when children are young.
- Keep chairs, tables, beds, and climbable objects away from windows and balconies.
- Install appropriate window locks and guards. Do not rely on insect screens to prevent falls.
- Secure wardrobes, bookshelves, televisions, and other heavy furniture to walls.
- Use corner and edge protectors on sharp furniture where necessary.
- Keep floors dry, organize electrical cords, and remove obstacles from walkways.
- Keep plastic bags, long cords, looped curtain cords, and small objects out of children's reach.
- For babies, maintain a clear and safe sleep space without pillows, loose blankets, stuffed toys, or other loose items in the crib.

Never leave a young child unattended on a bed, sofa, or changing table, even for a very short time.

### 2.2. Kitchen: Prevent burns, cuts, and poisoning

The kitchen contains heat sources, sharp objects, and chemicals. To protect children:

- Keep children away from active cooking areas and hot liquids or oil.
- Use rear stove burners whenever possible and turn pot handles inward.
- Keep kettles, rice cookers, electrical cords, and hot drinks away from table edges.
- Never carry a child while holding hot food or liquids.
- Store knives, scissors, sharp tools, and lighters in locked drawers or cabinets.
- Do not allow children to use kitchen appliances without appropriate supervision and guidance.
- Keep hot food, boiling water, and hot oil out of children's reach.

When feeding children, ensure they are seated securely, supervised by an adult, and given food prepared appropriately for their age and chewing ability.

### 2.3. Bathroom: Prevent drowning and slips

Children can drown even in relatively small amounts of water. Therefore:

- Stay within arm's reach whenever your child is bathing or near water.
- Never leave a child alone in a bathtub, even when using a bath seat or support device.
- Empty buckets, basins, and bathtubs after use and store them out of children's reach.
- Keep the bathroom door closed when it is not in use.
- Use non-slip mats and dry wet floors after bathing.
- Check the water temperature before bathing your child to prevent scalds.
- Keep hair dryers, electric shavers, and other electrical appliances away from bathtubs and water.

Remember: Bath seats and flotation aids do not replace direct adult supervision.

### 2.4. Stairs, balconies, and windows: Prevent falls from heights

Falls from heights can cause serious injuries. Parents should:

- Install sturdy safety gates that fit the staircase.
- Keep stairs well lit and free of toys and other objects.
- Install suitable railings that prevent children from slipping through or getting their heads trapped.
- Keep chairs, tables, plant pots, and other climbable objects away from balcony railings.
- Install child-resistant locks on balcony doors and windows.
- Never allow children to play alone on balconies, rooftops, or near open windows.

Ordinary insect screens are not designed to prevent children from falling.

### 2.5. Medicine and chemical storage: Prevent poisoning

Household medicines and chemicals can cause serious poisoning if children swallow, inhale, or come into direct contact with them.

- Store medicines, vitamins, cosmetics, detergents, pesticides, and chemicals in locked cabinets.
- Keep these products high and out of children's reach, preferably with additional safety locks.
- Keep products in their original packaging with their labels intact.
- Never transfer chemicals into drinking-water bottles, soft-drink bottles, or food containers.
- Do not call medicine candy or food to encourage children to take it.
- Never give children medicine without appropriate medical instructions.
- Check visitors' and family members' bags, which may contain medicines or other dangerous objects.

Child-resistant packaging does not replace safe storage.

### 2.6. Electrical outlets and appliances: Prevent electric shocks

To reduce electrical risks:

- Use tamper-resistant electrical outlets or suitable outlet covers.
- Keep exposed wires, damaged sockets, and faulty appliances out of children's reach.
- Avoid overloading electrical outlets.
- Never let children handle electrical appliances with wet hands or while standing on wet floors.
- Unplug unused appliances and keep electrical cords organized.
- Do not allow children to play with charging cables, outlets, or insert metal objects into sockets.
- Inspect your home's electrical system regularly.

If you find exposed wires or damaged equipment, safely disconnect the power and arrange repairs before using them again.

## 3. Prevent choking and suffocation during eating and play

Choking is a significant risk for young children, particularly during the first few years of life.

Parents should:

- Have children sit upright while eating. Do not let them eat while running, playing, or lying down.
- Prepare food in sizes and textures appropriate for the child's age and chewing ability.
- Avoid choking hazards such as whole nuts, hard candy, popcorn, round slices of sausage, whole grapes, and hard, round, or slippery food pieces.
- Supervise children throughout meals.
- Choose age-appropriate toys without small detachable parts.
- Keep button batteries, small magnets, coins, beads, and other small objects out of reach.
- Keep plastic bags, strings, and looped cords away from children's sleeping and play areas.

Button batteries and small magnets are particularly dangerous if swallowed. If you suspect your child has swallowed either, seek emergency medical care immediately, even if there are no obvious symptoms.

## 4. Preventing accidents according to your child's age

Different developmental stages bring different risks. Parents should adjust safety measures according to their child's mobility and understanding.

| Age | Common risks | Prevention measures |
|---|---|---|
| 0–6 months | Sleep-related suffocation, falls from beds, hot-water burns | Provide a safe sleep environment, never leave the baby unattended on an elevated surface, and check water temperature |
| 6–12 months | Choking, falls, collisions, swallowing small objects | Remove small objects, install safety gates, and supervise crawling and standing |
| 1–3 years | Burns, poisoning, drowning, falls, electric shocks | Lock medicine and chemical cabinets, block hazardous areas, and provide close supervision |
| 3–5 years | Falls, burns, choking, accidents while exploring | Teach simple safety rules and prevent unsupervised access to kitchens and electrical appliances |
| 6–11 years | Play-related injuries and accidents involving appliances or tools | Teach safe appliance use, establish household rules, and encourage self-protection skills |

Even when children can walk, talk, and understand instructions, they still need supervision because they may not accurately judge risks.

## 5. What should parents do if a child has an accident at home?

If an accident occurs, stay calm, make sure the area is safe for both you and your child, and assess the situation before providing appropriate first aid.

### If your child falls

- Move your child away from danger if it is safe to do so.
- Check their alertness, movement, pain, and any bleeding.
- Do not move your child unnecessarily if you suspect a neck or spinal injury, unless they are in immediate danger.
- Seek emergency medical care if your child loses consciousness, has a seizure, vomits repeatedly, develops worsening headaches, becomes confused or unusually sleepy, is difficult to wake, has severe bleeding, or shows unusual symptoms after a head injury.

### If your child gets burned

- Move your child away from the heat source.
- Cool the affected area under cool running water for approximately 20 minutes.
- Remove nearby clothing or jewelry unless it is stuck to the skin.
- Do not pull off clothing that is stuck to the burn.
- Do not apply ice, toothpaste, oil, butter, or traditional remedies.
- After cooling, loosely cover the burn with a clean, non-stick covering.

Seek medical assessment for a child's burn. Large or deep burns, burns involving the face, hands, or genitals, and burns caused by electricity or chemicals require urgent medical attention.

### If your child is choking

If your child is coughing forcefully and can breathe, encourage them to keep coughing while watching closely.

If your child cannot cough effectively, speak, or breathe, this is an emergency. Call emergency services and provide age-appropriate choking first aid. Infants under one year require back blows and chest thrusts. Children over one year may require back blows and abdominal thrusts using the correct first-aid technique.

Do not blindly put your fingers into your child's throat, as this may push the object further down. Parents should consider taking a pediatric first-aid course to learn these techniques correctly.

### If you suspect poisoning

- Move your child away from chemicals or toxic fumes if it is safe to do so.
- Keep the packaging, container, or name of the substance involved.
- Do not induce vomiting.
- Do not give milk, lemon juice, medicine, or chemical neutralizers unless instructed by a healthcare professional.
- Contact a medical facility or seek emergency care immediately if you suspect your child has swallowed a dangerous medicine or chemical.

Do not wait for symptoms to appear before seeking help. Some toxic substances can cause damage before obvious symptoms develop.

### If your child receives an electric shock

- Do not touch your child while the electrical source is still connected.
- Switch off the circuit breaker or power supply before approaching.
- If the power cannot be disconnected safely, do not try to pull the child away by hand. Call emergency services and seek professional help.
- Once the area is safe, check your child's responsiveness and breathing.
- Call emergency services if your child is unconscious, has difficulty breathing, experiences seizures, or shows unusual symptoms. Electric shocks can cause internal injuries even when there are no obvious external burns.

### If your child is drowning

- Remove your child from the water only if you can do so safely.
- Call emergency services immediately.
- Check responsiveness and breathing. If your child is not breathing normally, begin CPR if you are trained and follow emergency call-handler instructions.
- Do not turn your child upside down or try to force water out of their lungs.

Drowning is a medical emergency, even if your child appears to recover after being removed from the water.

**In Vietnam, call 115 if your child has a life-threatening emergency.** If you are unsure how to provide first aid, follow the emergency call handler's instructions.

## 6. Essential principles for making your home safer

Parents can regularly inspect their homes using these principles:

1. **See the home from your child's perspective:** Get down to your child's eye level to identify small objects, electrical outlets, cords, and dangerous items within reach.
2. **Remove hazards instead of relying only on warnings:** Do not simply tell children to stay away from knives, medicines, or electrical outlets. Store, cover, and secure dangerous items and areas.
3. **Provide direct supervision:** When children are near water, cooking areas, stairs, or other hazards, an adult should remain close and pay continuous attention.
4. **Inspect the home regularly:** When your child starts crawling, walking, climbing, or opening doors, reassess the entire home. Areas that were previously safe may become hazardous.
5. **Establish consistent household rules:** All caregivers should know where medicines and chemicals are stored, how to secure doors, and what to do in an emergency.
6. **Learn basic first aid:** Adults should learn how to respond to choking, burns, bleeding, and pediatric cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).

No home can be completely accident-proof. The goal is to reduce risks as much as possible and limit the consequences if an accident occurs.

## 7. Frequently asked questions

**Can a child drown in a bucket or basin of water?**

Yes. A young child can drown in a relatively small amount of water if they fall face down and cannot get out. Empty containers after use and never leave children alone near water.

**Should all electrical outlets have safety covers?**

Use tamper-resistant outlets or suitable protective devices, and keep damaged electrical equipment and cords out of reach. Covers should be securely installed and should not create small detachable parts that children could swallow.

**Is teaching children to avoid danger enough?**

No. Young children cannot fully assess risks and may forget instructions when curious or distracted. A more effective approach combines education, supervision, and changes to the home environment.

**If my child falls but continues playing normally, should I still monitor them?**

Yes. Continue observing your child after a fall, particularly if they hit their head. Seek emergency care if they develop repeated vomiting, worsening headaches, confusion, seizures, difficulty waking, or unusual behavior.

## Conclusion

Preventing household accidents starts with small but important changes: locking away medicines and chemicals, protecting electrical outlets, installing stair gates, keeping children away from hot cooking areas, removing choking hazards, and never leaving children alone near water.

Parents cannot prevent every accident, but they can significantly reduce the risks by creating a safer environment, providing appropriate supervision, and learning basic first aid.

*This article is intended for general safety education and accident prevention. It does not replace hands-on first-aid training or professional medical advice. If your child has a serious accident or develops unusual symptoms, contact emergency services and seek medical care immediately.*
